# Quantum Foundations

Classical physics explains an enormous range of motion, fields, and waves, but early twentieth-century experiments revealed sharp limits to that picture. Light sometimes behaves as if it arrives in packets of energy. Electrons sometimes produce interference patterns that look unmistakably wave-like. Quantum mechanics is the framework that keeps both stories true at once.

## Where the Classical Picture Breaks

Two experiments are especially useful starting points:

- In the **photoelectric effect**, light ejects electrons from a metal only when the light frequency is high enough. Brighter low-frequency light does not compensate for a frequency that is too small.
- In the **double-slit experiment**, even single particles build up an interference pattern over time, as though each detection is guided by a wave-like probability rule.

Those two facts push in opposite directions if we insist on a purely classical model. The photoelectric effect says light carries energy in discrete packets,

$$
E = hf,
$$

while the double-slit experiment says matter can display interference, diffraction, and other wave phenomena.

## Photons, Thresholds, and the Photoelectric Effect

Einstein's photoelectric explanation treats light as photons, each carrying one energy packet $E = hf$. A metal surface needs a minimum energy called the **work function** $\Phi$ to remove one electron. Any leftover energy appears as the emitted electron's kinetic energy:

$$
K_{\max} = hf - \Phi.
$$

The key prediction is that **frequency** controls whether electrons are emitted at all, while **intensity** mainly controls how many photons arrive each second. That is why dim ultraviolet light can eject electrons while bright red light can fail completely.

## Matter Waves

The second clue comes from particles that act wave-like. De Broglie proposed that matter with momentum $p$ has an associated wavelength

$$
\lambda = \frac{h}{p}.
$$

That relation connects quantum behavior back to the waves module. Shorter wavelengths usually mean smaller visible interference features, while larger wavelengths spread fringes farther apart. For everyday objects the wavelength is absurdly tiny, so the wave behavior is effectively invisible. For electrons and other microscopic particles, it becomes measurable.

## Single Particles and Interference

Quantum mechanics does **not** say that a particle smears out like classical dust. Instead, it says the experiment is governed by a complex amplitude whose squared magnitude gives probabilities. In a double-slit setup, amplitudes associated with the two paths combine before probabilities are computed. That is why the screen shows alternating bright and dark bands rather than two smooth piles.

As you explore the screen below, try this sequence:

1. Leave coherence high and watch the fringe pattern emerge from single detections.
2. Increase the wavelength and compare the spacing between neighboring bright bands.
3. Increase the slit separation and notice the fringes squeeze closer together.
4. Reduce the coherence and watch the dark gaps fill in toward a classical-looking envelope.

  <WorkedExampleCard
    title="Check Whether Light Can Eject Electrons"
    accent="blue"
  >
    #### Problem

    A metal has work function $\Phi = 2.2\,\text{eV}$. Light of frequency $f = 7.5 \times 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$ shines on the surface. Using $h = 4.14 \times 10^{-15}\,\text{eV}\cdot\text{s}$, determine whether electrons are emitted and find the maximum kinetic energy if they are.

    #### Given

    - $\Phi = 2.2\,\text{eV}$
    - $f = 7.5 \times 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$
    - $h = 4.14 \times 10^{-15}\,\text{eV}\cdot\text{s}$

    #### Use

    $$K_{\max} = hf - \Phi.$$

    #### Solve

    First compute the photon energy:

    $$
    hf = (4.14 \times 10^{-15})(7.5 \times 10^{14}) \approx 3.11\,\text{eV}.
    $$

    Then subtract the work function:

    $$
    K_{\max} = 3.11 - 2.2 = 0.91\,\text{eV}.
    $$

    Since the photon energy exceeds the work function, electrons are emitted.

    #### Check

    The result is positive, so the photons have enough energy to free electrons and still leave some kinetic energy behind.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard
    title="Estimate An Electron's De Broglie Wavelength"
    accent="red"
  >
    #### Problem

    An electron has momentum $p = 6.6 \times 10^{-24}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$. Estimate its de Broglie wavelength using $h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\cdot\text{s}$.

    #### Given

    - $p = 6.6 \times 10^{-24}\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$
    - $h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\cdot\text{s}$

    #### Use

    $$\lambda = \frac{h}{p}.$$

    #### Solve

    $$
    \lambda = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{6.6 \times 10^{-24}}
    \approx 1.00 \times 10^{-10}\,\text{m}.
    $$

    So the wavelength is about
    $$
    0.10\,\text{nm}.
    $$

    #### Check

    A sub-nanometer wavelength is microscopic but not absurdly tiny, which is why electron diffraction can be observed in atomic-scale experiments.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

# Angular Kinematics

A spinning wheel is a strange object to describe with the kinematics of [straight-line motion](/kinematics). Every piece of it is moving, but no two pieces share the same velocity — the rim races along while material near the axle barely crawls. The trick is to stop tracking positions and start tracking a single **angle**. One number, $\theta$, pins down the orientation of the whole wheel, and everything else follows from it.

## Radians: the Natural Angle

Wrap a distance around a circle and you get the natural definition of angle: the **radian**. An angle in radians is the arc length it spans divided by the radius,

$$
\theta = \frac{s}{r} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad s = r\theta.
$$

One full turn wraps the full circumference $2\pi r$, so a revolution is $2\pi$ radians and a radian is about $57.3°$. Degrees and revolutions are fine for bookkeeping, but $s = r\theta$ — and every formula descended from it — only works when $\theta$ is in radians. It is worth being paranoid about this; it is the single most common error in rotation problems.

## The Angular Vocabulary

Each linear quantity has a rotational twin, built the same way:

| Linear | Angular | Connection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| position $x$ | angle $\theta$ (rad) | $s = r\theta$ |
| velocity $v = \dfrac{dx}{dt}$ | angular velocity $\omega = \dfrac{d\theta}{dt}$ (rad/s) | $v = r\omega$ |
| acceleration $a = \dfrac{dv}{dt}$ | angular acceleration $\alpha = \dfrac{d\omega}{dt}$ (rad/s²) | $a_t = r\alpha$ |

The connections in the last column are what make the angular description powerful. A rigid body has just *one* $\omega$ — every point sweeps the same angle in the same time — but each point's speed grows with its distance from the axis:

$$
v = r\omega.
$$

That is why the rim of a merry-go-round feels fast while the center feels calm: same $\omega$, different $r$.

Signs work like they do on a number line, except the line is bent into a circle: pick a positive rotation direction (counterclockwise, by convention), and $\omega$ and $\alpha$ carry signs relative to it. A wheel spinning counterclockwise ($\omega > 0$) while slowing down has a *clockwise* angular acceleration ($\alpha < 0$) — just as a car moving in the $+x$ direction while braking has a negative $a$.

One caution: $a_t = r\alpha$ is only the **tangential** part of a point's acceleration — the part that changes its speed. Any point moving in a circle *also* has the inward centripetal part $a_c = r\omega^2$, even at constant $\omega$. The [rotating frames](/rotation/rotating-frames) page takes that inward piece apart in detail.

## The Same Old Equations, Rotated

Because $\theta$, $\omega$, and $\alpha$ are defined exactly like $x$, $v$, and $a$, constant-$\alpha$ rotation obeys the same kinematic equations with the letters swapped:

$$
\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t, \qquad
\theta = \theta_0 + \omega_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}\alpha t^2, \qquad
\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\,\Delta\theta.
$$

No new problem-solving skills are required — identify what is given, spot the missing variable, and pick the equation that avoids it, exactly as in linear kinematics.

  <WorkedExampleCard title="From RPM to Radians per Second" accent="blue">
    #### Problem

    An old hard drive spins at $300\ \text{rpm}$. What is its angular speed in rad/s, and how fast does a point at radius $4.0\ \text{cm}$ move?

    #### Use

    One revolution is $2\pi$ radians, so $\omega = 2\pi \cdot \text{rpm}/60$; then $v = r\omega$.

    #### Solve

    $$
    \omega = \frac{2\pi (300)}{60} = 10\pi \approx 31.4\ \text{rad/s},
    $$

    $$
    v = r\omega = (0.040)(31.4) \approx 1.3\ \text{m/s}.
    $$

    #### Check

    Five revolutions per second times roughly $6.28$ radians per revolution is indeed about $31$ rad/s. The units only work because radians are dimensionless — a speed in "degrees·meters per second" would mean nothing.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard title="Counting Revolutions During Spin-Up" accent="red">
    #### Problem

    A turbine starts from rest and accelerates at a constant $\alpha = 2.0\ \text{rad/s}^2$ for $10\ \text{s}$. Find its final angular speed and the number of revolutions it completes.

    #### Use

    Constant-$\alpha$ kinematics from rest: $\omega = \alpha t$ and $\theta = \tfrac{1}{2}\alpha t^2$.

    #### Solve

    $$
    \omega = (2.0)(10) = 20\ \text{rad/s},
    $$

    $$
    \theta = \tfrac{1}{2}(2.0)(10)^2 = 100\ \text{rad}
    \quad\Rightarrow\quad
    \frac{100}{2\pi} \approx 15.9\ \text{revolutions}.
    $$

    #### Check

    The average angular speed is $10\ \text{rad/s}$ (half the final value), and $10\ \text{rad/s} \times 10\ \text{s} = 100\ \text{rad}$ — the two routes agree. You can replay these exact numbers in the grapher above.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

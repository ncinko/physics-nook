# Electric Potential

The **electric potential** describes electric interactions in terms of *energy* rather than *force*. Just as gravitational potential energy relates to the gravitational force, electric potential energy relates to the electric force. When a charge moves through a field, its potential energy changes; we define the electric potential $V$ as the potential energy per unit charge.



## Potential Energy Landscapes

Visualizing an object "rolling" on a potential energy surface connects the force and energy pictures. Below, a mass feels either a spring-like force or simple gravity. In both cases it tends to move *downhill* along the 1D potential energy curve, trading potential energy for kinetic energy and back.

This downhill tendency is a general property of conservative forces. The force always points toward decreasing potential energy, captured in one dimension by

$$
F \approx -\frac{\Delta U}{\Delta x},
$$

which is the slope of the potential energy function $U(x)$.

<DerivationPanel title="Which forces have a potential energy?">

The work done by a **conservative** force depends only on the start and end positions, not the path taken. Those are exactly the forces for which we can define a scalar potential energy. The electric, gravitational, and spring forces are conservative; friction and air resistance are not.

In one dimension, the work done over a small step is $W \approx F\,\Delta x$. For a conservative force we call this a change in potential energy, $\Delta U = -W$, so

$$
F \approx -\frac{\Delta U}{\Delta x}.
$$

In three dimensions the same idea is the gradient, which points in the direction of steepest increase of a scalar field:

$$
\vec{\mathbf F} = -\nabla U = -\left\langle \frac{\partial U}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial U}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial U}{\partial z} \right\rangle.
$$

Don't worry if that notation is unfamiliar — picture a 2D energy landscape $U(x,y)$ of hills and valleys, with matter tending to flow downhill.

</DerivationPanel>

## From Energy to Potential

Before the electrical case, recall gravitational potential energy near Earth's surface, $U = mgh$.

<DerivationPanel title="Does U = mgh agree with our definition?">

Yes. With "up" positive, the gravitational force near the surface is $F = -mg$. The work done moving an object up a small height $\Delta h$ is $W = F\,\Delta h = -mg\,\Delta h$, so

$$
\Delta U = -W = mg\,\Delta h.
$$

Choosing $U = 0$ at $h = 0$ gives $U(h) = mgh$.

</DerivationPanel>

Suppose we want the potential energy *independent of mass*. Define $V = gh$ with the rule $U = mV$. This $V$ (units of J/kg) tells us the potential energy per unit mass at a given height.

For the electric force we do exactly this. The electric potential $V$ is defined so that

$$
U = qV,
$$

where $q$ is the charge. Its units are J/C, which we call **volts**. Only *changes* in potential energy — and therefore changes in potential — are physical: $V$ can be shifted by any constant without changing the physics, just as elevation does not depend on our choice of sea level. Picking that constant is choosing a "zero of potential."

## Point Charge Potential

The electric potential due to a point charge $Q$ is

$$
V = k\,\frac{Q}{r},
$$

where $r$ is the distance from the charge and $k = 9 \times 10^9\ \tfrac{\text{N·m}^2}{\text{C}^2}$.

<DerivationPanel title="Where does V = kQ/r come from?">

Start from $V = \tfrac{U}{q}$, so we need the potential energy of a charge $q$ in the field of $Q$. Coulomb's law gives the force $F = k\dfrac{Qq}{r^2}$. The change in potential energy bringing $q$ from infinity to $r$ is

$$
\Delta U = -\int_{\infty}^{r} k\,\frac{Qq}{r^2}\,dr = -kQq\left[-\frac{1}{r}\right]_{\infty}^{r} = k\,\frac{Qq}{r}.
$$

Dividing by $q$,

$$
V = \frac{U}{q} = k\,\frac{Q}{r}.
$$

</DerivationPanel>

We can picture $V(x,y)$ with a color map. Below, brighter colors are higher potential; it decreases as you move away from the positive charge, since $V \propto 1/r$. Drag the charge, and toggle the equipotential rings — they are circles of constant $V$.

In 3D we draw *equipotential surfaces*, the analog of these rings. Because $U = qV$, positive and negative charges behave differently in the *same* potential: both tend toward lower *potential energy*, not lower *potential*. When the signs get confusing, fall back on "like charges repel, opposites attract."

Notice that the equipotential lines are everywhere perpendicular to the field lines. Just as $F \approx -\tfrac{\Delta U}{\Delta x}$ in one dimension, the field is the (negative) slope of the potential:

$$
E \approx -\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta x}.
$$

<ProblemSolvingSection title="Practice">
  <WorkedExampleCard title="Potential of a Point Charge" accent="blue">
    #### Problem

    What is the electric potential $0.25\,\text{m}$ from a $+3\,\mu\text{C}$ point charge?

    #### Solve

    $$
    V = k\,\frac{q}{r} = 9\times10^9 \cdot \frac{3\times10^{-6}}{0.25} \approx 1.1\times10^5\,\text{V}.
    $$
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard title="Work Along an Equipotential" accent="blue">
    #### Problem

    Why is no work required to move a charge along an equipotential surface?

    #### Reasoning

    Along an equipotential, $\Delta V = 0$. Since $\Delta U = q\,\Delta V$, the change in potential energy is zero — and the work done by the electric force equals $-\Delta U = 0$.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard title="Change in Potential Energy" accent="red">
    #### Problem

    Find the change in electric potential energy of a $-3\,\mu\text{C}$ charge that moves from $5\,\text{V}$ to $12\,\text{V}$.

    #### Solve

    With $\Delta V = 7\,\text{V}$ and $q = -3\times10^{-6}\,\text{C}$,

    $$
    \Delta U = q\,\Delta V = (-3\times10^{-6})(7) = -2.1\times10^{-5}\,\text{J}.
    $$

    The negative charge *loses* potential energy moving toward higher potential.
  </WorkedExampleCard>
</ProblemSolvingSection>

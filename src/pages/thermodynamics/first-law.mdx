# Heat, Work, and the First Law

The foundations page treated internal energy as a property of the system's state. The next question is how that internal energy changes. Thermodynamics organizes those changes using two forms of energy transfer: **heat** and **work**.

Heat and work are not substances stored inside the system. They describe energy crossing the system boundary. Once the energy is inside, it contributes to the system's internal energy or leaves again through another transfer.

## Heat as Energy Transfer

Energy transferred because of a temperature difference is **heat**, written $Q$. In this path, we use the convention

- $Q > 0$ when heat is added to the system
- $Q < 0$ when heat leaves the system

Heat flow continues spontaneously from higher temperature to lower temperature until thermal equilibrium is reached.

## Work Done By the System

Work is energy transferred when a macroscopic force acts through a displacement. A gas pushing a piston is the classic thermodynamic example. This path uses one consistent sign convention:

$$
W = \text{work done by the system}.
$$

So expansion work is positive: the gas pushes the surroundings and gives energy away. Compression work is negative: the surroundings push on the gas and energy enters the system through work.

At constant external pressure, the boundary work is

$$
W = P\Delta V.
$$

If the volume increases, $\Delta V > 0$ and $W > 0$. If the volume decreases, $\Delta V < 0$ and $W < 0$.

## The First Law

The first law is energy conservation applied to a thermodynamic system:

$$
\Delta U = Q - W.
$$

This equation says the system's internal energy increases when heat is added and decreases when the system does work on the surroundings. With our convention, subtracting a negative work during compression correctly increases the internal energy.

## Common Processes

Some process labels make the first-law bookkeeping faster:

- **Constant volume:** $\Delta V = 0$, so $W = 0$ and $\Delta U = Q$.
- **Expansion at constant pressure:** $W = P\Delta V > 0$, so part of the added heat can leave as work.
- **Compression:** $\Delta V < 0$, so $W < 0$ and the work term increases $U$ because $\Delta U = Q - W$.

The same energy conservation law covers all of them. The main danger is mixing sign conventions halfway through a problem.

<ProblemSolvingSection>
  <WorkedExampleCard
    title="Constant-Volume Heating"
    accent="blue"
  >
    #### Problem

    A sealed rigid container of gas receives $Q = 450\,\text{J}$ of heat. The container does not change volume. Find $W$ and $\Delta U$.

    #### Given

    - $Q = +450\,\text{J}$
    - $\Delta V = 0$

    #### Use

    At constant volume,

    $$
    W = P\Delta V = 0.
    $$

    The first law is

    $$
    \Delta U = Q - W.
    $$

    #### Solve

    $$
    \Delta U = 450 - 0 = 450\,\text{J}.
    $$

    The internal energy increases by $450\,\text{J}$.

    #### Check

    Since the container is rigid, the gas does no expansion work. All the heat added stays in the gas as increased internal energy.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard
    title="Expansion Work at Constant Pressure"
    accent="red"
  >
    #### Problem

    A gas expands at constant pressure $P = 120\,\text{kPa}$ from $2.0\,\text{L}$ to $5.0\,\text{L}$ while absorbing $Q = 620\,\text{J}$ of heat. Find the work done by the gas and the change in internal energy.

    #### Given

    - $P = 120\,\text{kPa}$
    - $\Delta V = 5.0 - 2.0 = 3.0\,\text{L}$
    - $Q = +620\,\text{J}$

    #### Use

    Because $1\,\text{kPa}\cdot\text{L} = 1\,\text{J}$,

    $$
    W = P\Delta V.
    $$

    Then use

    $$
    \Delta U = Q - W.
    $$

    #### Solve

    $$
    W = (120)(3.0) = 360\,\text{J}.
    $$

    $$
    \Delta U = 620 - 360 = 260\,\text{J}.
    $$

    #### Check

    The gas absorbed $620\,\text{J}$, but $360\,\text{J}$ left as expansion work. The remaining $260\,\text{J}$ increased internal energy.
  </WorkedExampleCard>
</ProblemSolvingSection>

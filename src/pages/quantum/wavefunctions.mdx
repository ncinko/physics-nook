# Wavefunctions and Superposition

The experimental clues from quantum foundations need a mathematical language. That language is the **wavefunction** $\psi$. It is not a classical material wave, and it is not a direct probability by itself. Instead, it is an amplitude whose squared magnitude tells us how measurement outcomes are distributed.

## From Amplitude to Probability

For a normalized wavefunction, the probability density is

$$
P(x) = |\psi(x)|^2.
$$

That single rule matters more than almost anything else in introductory quantum mechanics. It says quantum predictions come from amplitudes first and probabilities second. Since amplitudes can interfere, the probability pattern can contain peaks, dips, and cancellations that a classical probability mixture would miss.

## Normalization

If a particle must be found somewhere, then the total probability must add to $1$. In continuous language, that becomes

$$
\int |\psi(x)|^2\,dx = 1.
$$

This condition is called **normalization**. It does not tell you the shape of the state by itself, but it fixes the overall scale. If you double the amplitude everywhere, the probability density becomes four times larger, so the state would no longer represent a valid total probability of $1$.

## Phase and Superposition

Quantum states can be added. If $\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$ are allowed states, then a new state can be built from

$$
\psi = a\phi_1 + b\phi_2.
$$

This is the principle of **superposition**. The coefficients $a$ and $b$ are amplitudes, not probabilities. Their phases matter because the sum is taken before the magnitude is squared. Two contributions can reinforce one another in one region and partly cancel in another.

That is why changing phase can alter the amplitude picture even when the state remains perfectly normalized.

## Measurement Language

Before a position measurement, the wavefunction carries the information needed to assign probabilities to different outcomes. After a measurement, the system is found at one specific outcome. Repeating the same experiment many times rebuilds the distribution encoded by $|\psi|^2$.

The same idea applies more generally: if a state is written as a combination of energy eigenstates, then the squared magnitudes of the coefficients give the probabilities for those energy outcomes.

  <WorkedExampleCard
    title="Normalize A Simple Box State"
    accent="blue"
  >
    #### Problem

    A particle in a one-dimensional box has wavefunction

    $$
    \psi(x) = A \sin\!\left(\frac{\pi x}{L}\right)
    $$

    for $0 \le x \le L$, and $\psi(x)=0$ elsewhere. Find the normalization constant $A$.

    #### Given

    - $\psi(x) = A\sin(\pi x/L)$ in the box
    - the state must satisfy $\int_0^L |\psi(x)|^2\,dx = 1$

    #### Use

    $$
    \int_0^L A^2 \sin^2\!\left(\frac{\pi x}{L}\right) dx = 1.
    $$

    Also,

    $$
    \int_0^L \sin^2\!\left(\frac{\pi x}{L}\right) dx = \frac{L}{2}.
    $$

    #### Solve

    Substitute the integral result:

    $$
    A^2 \frac{L}{2} = 1.
    $$

    So

    $$
    A^2 = \frac{2}{L}
    \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad
    A = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}.
    $$

    #### Check

    The amplitude scale depends on box width. A wider box spreads the same total probability over a larger region, so the normalized amplitude becomes smaller.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard
    title="Read Energy Probabilities From Coefficients"
    accent="red"
  >
    #### Problem

    A state is written as

    $$
    \psi = \sqrt{0.75}\,\phi_1 + e^{i\pi/3}\sqrt{0.25}\,\phi_2,
    $$

    where $\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$ are energy eigenstates. What are the probabilities of measuring the energies associated with $\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$?

    #### Given

    - coefficient of $\phi_1$: $\sqrt{0.75}$
    - coefficient of $\phi_2$: $e^{i\pi/3}\sqrt{0.25}$

    #### Use

    For an energy measurement in this basis, the probabilities are the squared magnitudes of the coefficients.

    #### Solve

    $$
    P(E_1) = \left|\sqrt{0.75}\right|^2 = 0.75,
    \qquad
    P(E_2) = \left|e^{i\pi/3}\sqrt{0.25}\right|^2 = 0.25.
    $$

    So the outcomes are

    - $75\%$ for the energy of $\phi_1$
    - $25\%$ for the energy of $\phi_2$

    #### Check

    The phase factor changes the amplitude but not the coefficient magnitude, so it does not change these basis-measurement probabilities.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

# SI Units & Scientific Notation

Most physical quantities require units alongside a numerical value.  $67\ \text{meters}$ is a length, $67\ \text{seconds}$ is a time, and $67\ \text{kilograms}$ is a mass.  The number $67$ (while it does have memetic meaning), does not have any physical meaning without a unit. 

A measurement has two parts:

$$
\text{measurement} = \text{number} \times \text{unit}.
$$

The number tells *how many*. The unit tells how many of *what*.  It is extremely useful, as notated above, to think of this as multiplication of the two.  As you perform arithmetic/algebra with measurements, the same operations apply to both the numerical values and corresponding units.

For example, if a car travels $120\ \text{miles}$ in $2\ \text{hours}$, then its average speed is

$$
v = \frac{120\ \text{miles}}{2\ \text{hours}} = 60\ \text{mi/hr}.
$$

As expected, miles per hour is a standard way of expressing speed.  If we instead had units of square miles, there was an error in our reasoning.  *Checking units* is often an easy way of spotting mistakes in your work.

## SI Units

The SI system (*Système international d'unités*) is the shared measuring language used across physics. There are relatively few base units, and most units are just combinations of these.

<SIUnitDefinitions client:visible />

Derived units are combinations of these base units: speed is measured in $\text{m/s}$ and acceleration in $\text{m/s}^2$.  Some combinations are given a special name: force is measured in newtons $\text{N} = \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2$, energy in joules $\text{J} = \text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$, and momentum (among a small group of people) in langs $\text{L} = \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$. The special names are convenient, but the base-unit structure often hints at the deeper relationships between quantities and how we define them.

## Powers of 10

Metric prefixes are shorthand powers of ten attached to a unit. A millimeter is not a new kind of length; it is $10^{-3}$ meters. A kilometer is $10^3$ meters. Replace the prefix with its power of ten, and the conversion becomes ordinary multiplication.

## Scientific Notation

Scientific notation writes a number as a coefficient times a power of ten:

$$
a \times 10^n,
\qquad 1 \le |a| < 10.
$$

This notation handles both Earth's radius, about $6.4 \times 10^6\ \text{m}$, and a visible-light wavelength, about $5.0 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}$, without long strings of zeros.


<InteractiveAnchor id="scientific-notation-slider" />

<ScientificNotationSlider client:visible />

This also happens to be a convenient way of expressing the precision of a measurement.  If a length is reported as

$$
3.10 \times 10^2\ \text{m},
$$

the final digit of the measurement is implied to be reliable (i.e. it's probably between 309 and 311 meters).  The numbers $3$, $1$, and the final $0$ in the coefficient are said to be *significant figures*.

That is why these are different claims:

<div className="my-6 overflow-x-auto [&_table]:!my-0 [&_table]:!w-max [&_th]:!px-4 [&_td]:!px-4">

| Value | Significant figures | Precision claim |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $3.1 \times 10^2\ \text{m}$ | 2 | nearest $10\ \text{m}$ |
| $3.10 \times 10^2\ \text{m}$ | 3 | nearest $1\ \text{m}$ |
| $3.100 \times 10^2\ \text{m}$ | 4 | nearest $0.1\ \text{m}$ |

  <WorkedExampleCard
    title="Convert a small length"
    accent="blue"
  >
    #### Problem

    A wire is $680\ \mu\text{m}$ thick. Write its thickness in meters and in scientific notation.

    #### Use

    Micro means $10^{-6}$:

    $$
    680\ \mu\text{m} = 680 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}.
    $$

    #### Solve

    $$
    680 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}
    = 0.000680\ \text{m}
    = 6.80 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m}.
    $$

    #### Check

    Micrometers are much smaller than meters, so the meter value should be a small decimal. The trailing zero in $6.80$ preserves the three significant figures from $680$.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard
    title="Compare two distances"
    accent="blue"
  >
    #### Problem

    Which is larger: $3.2 \times 10^5\ \text{m}$ or $7.5 \times 10^4\ \text{m}$?

    #### Solve

    Compare powers first. Since $10^5$ is ten times larger than $10^4$, rewrite the second value with the same power:

    $$
    7.5 \times 10^4\ \text{m}
    = 0.75 \times 10^5\ \text{m}.
    $$

    Now compare coefficients:

    $$
    3.2 \times 10^5\ \text{m} > 0.75 \times 10^5\ \text{m}.
    $$

    #### Check

    In ordinary notation these are $320{,}000\ \text{m}$ and $75{,}000\ \text{m}$, so the first distance is larger.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard
    title="Report a measured result"
    accent="blue"
  >
    #### Problem

    A distance calculation gives $0.004782\ \text{m}$ with an uncertainty of $0.00031\ \text{m}$. Report the result in scientific notation.

    #### Use

    Round the uncertainty to one significant figure, then match the value to that place:

    $$
    0.00031\ \text{m} \rightarrow 0.0003\ \text{m}.
    $$

    #### Solve

    The value should stop in the same decimal place:

    $$
    0.004782\ \text{m} \rightarrow 0.0048\ \text{m}.
    $$

    Now factor out $10^{-3}\ \text{m}$ from both parts:

    $$
    0.0048 \pm 0.0003\ \text{m}
    = (4.8 \pm 0.3) \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}.
    $$

    #### Check

    The uncertainty reaches the tenths place in the coefficient, so the measured value also stops at the tenths place: $4.8 \pm 0.3$.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

# Torque and Moment of Inertia

Push a door at its handle and it swings open; push with the same force next to the hinge and it barely moves. [Forces](/forces) alone cannot explain the difference — the force was identical both times. What changed is *where* and *in what direction* it was applied. Rotation needs its own version of force, and its own version of mass to resist it.

## Torque: the Turning Effectiveness of a Force

The rotational effectiveness of a force is its **torque**,

$$
\tau = rF\sin\theta,
$$

where $r$ is the distance from the pivot to the point where the force acts, and $\theta$ is the angle between the force and the line from the pivot. Two things kill a torque: applying the force close to the pivot (small $r$) and aiming it along the line to the pivot ($\sin\theta = 0$ — all push, no turn).

There is a useful way to read the same formula geometrically. Slide the force along its own line of action and nothing about its turning ability changes; what matters is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to that line, called the **lever arm** $r_\perp = r\sin\theta$. Then $\tau = r_\perp F$: torque is force times lever arm.

Torque carries a sign — counterclockwise is positive by convention — and the SI unit is the newton-meter ($\text{N·m}$). This is why a longer wrench loosens a stubborn bolt: the same hand force acting on a longer lever arm produces a larger torque.

## Moment of Inertia: Where the Mass Sits

In the linear world, mass measures how much an object resists acceleration. Its rotational twin is the **moment of inertia** $I$ — but with a twist: it depends not just on how much mass there is, but on *how far from the axis* it sits. For a single point mass,

$$
I = mr^2,
$$

and for a collection of masses, $I = \sum_i m_i r_i^2$. The $r^2$ is the punchline. Moving a mass twice as far from the axis makes it *four times* harder to spin up. Two objects with identical mass can have wildly different moments of inertia.

Summing $mr^2$ over every particle of a solid shape gives compact results for the common cases (mass $m$, outer radius $r$, rod length $L$):

| Shape (axis through center) | Moment of inertia |
| --- | --- |
| thin hoop | $mr^2$ |
| solid disk or cylinder | $\tfrac{1}{2}mr^2$ |
| solid sphere | $\tfrac{2}{5}mr^2$ |
| hollow sphere | $\tfrac{2}{3}mr^2$ |
| thin rod, axis through center | $\tfrac{1}{12}mL^2$ |
| thin rod, axis through end | $\tfrac{1}{3}mL^2$ |

The ordering makes physical sense: a hoop keeps all of its mass at the full radius, so its coefficient is the largest possible; a solid sphere buries most of its mass near the axis and comes in lowest.

## Newton's Second Law for Rotation

Put the two new quantities together and the familiar law reappears in rotational costume:

$$
\vec F = m\vec a \quad\longrightarrow\quad \tau_{\text{net}} = I\alpha.
$$

A net torque produces angular acceleration, in inverse proportion to the moment of inertia. Every intuition from $F = ma$ transfers: double the torque, double the angular acceleration; double the moment of inertia, halve it.

<ProblemSolvingSection title="Problem Solving">
  <WorkedExampleCard title="Torque on a Stubborn Bolt" accent="blue">
    #### Problem

    A hand applies $40\ \text{N}$ to a wrench at $r = 0.24\ \text{m}$ from the bolt, at $60°$ to the handle. What torque does it produce? What is the best angle?

    #### Use

    $\tau = rF\sin\theta$; torque is maximized when the force is perpendicular to the handle.

    #### Solve

    $$
    \tau = (0.24)(40)\sin 60° = (0.24)(40)(0.866) \approx 8.3\ \text{N·m}.
    $$

    At $\theta = 90°$ the same force would give $\tau = (0.24)(40) = 9.6\ \text{N·m}$.

    #### Check

    These are the default settings of the explorer above — drag the angle to $90°$ and watch the lever arm grow to the full $0.24\ \text{m}$. Pushing at $60°$ wastes $\cos 60° = 50\%$ of the force along the handle, but only costs $13\%$ of the torque, because $\sin 60° = 0.87$.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard title="Atwood Machine with a Real Pulley" accent="red">
    #### Problem

    Masses $m_1 = 1.0\ \text{kg}$ and $m_2 = 1.5\ \text{kg}$ hang from a string over a solid-disk pulley of mass $M = 1.0\ \text{kg}$ and radius $R = 0.10\ \text{m}$. Find the acceleration. (The string does not slip on the pulley.)

    #### Use

    Newton's second law on each mass, $\tau = I\alpha$ on the pulley with $I = \tfrac{1}{2}MR^2$, and the no-slip link $a = R\alpha$. The two string tensions are *different* — their difference is what torques the pulley.

    #### Solve

    Adding the three equations, the tensions cancel and every term of $I/R^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}M$ acts like extra mass to accelerate:

    $$
    a = \frac{(m_2 - m_1)g}{m_1 + m_2 + \tfrac{1}{2}M}
      = \frac{(0.5)(9.8)}{1.0 + 1.5 + 0.5}
      = 1.6\ \text{m/s}^2.
    $$

    #### Check

    With a massless pulley the answer would be $(0.5)(9.8)/2.5 = 2.0\ \text{m/s}^2$ — the pulley's inertia slows the system, exactly as adding $\tfrac{1}{2}M$ of ordinary mass would. In the limit $M \to 0$ the familiar Atwood result returns.
  </WorkedExampleCard>
</ProblemSolvingSection>

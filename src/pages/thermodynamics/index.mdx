# Thermodynamics Foundations

Thermodynamics is the physics of energy when many microscopic pieces are involved. A single molecule has a position and velocity. A cup of gas has far too many molecules to track one by one, so we describe it using macroscopic quantities: temperature, pressure, volume, and internal energy.

The move is not a retreat from mechanics. It is a change in viewpoint. Instead of predicting every microscopic detail, we ask which large-scale properties are stable, measurable, and useful.

## Systems and Surroundings

A **system** is the part of the universe we choose to study. Everything outside it is the **surroundings**. The boundary between them can be real, like the walls of a container, or imaginary, like a drawn surface around a region of air.

Thermodynamic systems are often classified by what can cross that boundary:

- An **open system** can exchange matter and energy with its surroundings.
- A **closed system** can exchange energy but not matter.
- An **isolated system** exchanges neither matter nor energy.

The classification is a modeling choice. A sealed metal can of gas is closed if we care about the gas molecules staying inside, but it is not isolated if heat can flow through the metal wall.

## State Variables

A thermodynamic **state** is described by variables that do not depend on the detailed path used to reach that state. Common state variables include

$$
P,\quad V,\quad T,\quad U.
$$

Here $P$ is pressure, $V$ is volume, $T$ is temperature, and $U$ is internal energy. For a simple ideal gas, these variables are connected by the ideal gas law,

$$
PV = nRT,
$$

where $n$ is the number of moles and $R$ is the gas constant.

## Thermal Equilibrium and Temperature

Two systems are in **thermal equilibrium** when they can exchange energy as heat, but no net heat flow occurs. Temperature is the state variable that tells us the direction of spontaneous heat flow: energy flows as heat from higher temperature to lower temperature until thermal equilibrium is reached.

Microscopically, temperature is not the speed of one molecule. It is a distribution-level quantity. In a gas at one temperature, some particles are slow, some are fast, and a few are very fast. Raising the temperature shifts the whole speed distribution upward.

For an ideal monatomic gas, the average translational kinetic energy per particle is

$$
\langle K \rangle = \frac{3}{2}k_B T.
$$

The angle brackets mean an average over many particles, and $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant.

## Internal Energy

The **internal energy** $U$ is the total microscopic energy stored inside the system: translational kinetic energy, rotational and vibrational energy, molecular binding energy, and other microscopic contributions. It is not the same as the kinetic energy of the whole object moving through space.

For an ideal monatomic gas, there is no intermolecular potential energy in the model, so internal energy depends only on temperature:

$$
U = \frac{3}{2}nRT.
$$

That simplicity is special to the ideal-gas model, but it gives a clean starting point: temperature tracks average microscopic energy, and internal energy tracks the total microscopic store.

  <WorkedExampleCard
    title="Convert Temperature Scales"
    accent="blue"
  >
    #### Problem

    A laboratory gas sample is at $25^\circ\text{C}$. Convert this temperature to kelvin and Fahrenheit.

    #### Given

    - $T_C = 25^\circ\text{C}$

    #### Use

    $$
    T_K = T_C + 273.15,
    \qquad
    T_F = \frac{9}{5}T_C + 32.
    $$

    #### Solve

    $$
    T_K = 25 + 273.15 = 298.15\,\text{K}.
    $$

    $$
    T_F = \frac{9}{5}(25) + 32 = 45 + 32 = 77^\circ\text{F}.
    $$

    #### Check

    Room temperature should be near $300\,\text{K}$ and near $77^\circ\text{F}$, so both conversions are reasonable.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard
    title="Compare Average Kinetic Energies"
    accent="red"
  >
    #### Problem

    Compare the average translational kinetic energy per molecule for an ideal gas at $300\,\text{K}$ and $600\,\text{K}$.

    #### Given

    - $T_1 = 300\,\text{K}$
    - $T_2 = 600\,\text{K}$

    #### Use

    $$
    \langle K \rangle = \frac{3}{2}k_B T.
    $$

    Since $k_B$ and $\frac{3}{2}$ are the same in both cases,

    $$
    \frac{\langle K_2 \rangle}{\langle K_1 \rangle}
    =
    \frac{T_2}{T_1}.
    $$

    #### Solve

    $$
    \frac{\langle K_2 \rangle}{\langle K_1 \rangle}
    =
    \frac{600}{300}
    = 2.
    $$

    The average translational kinetic energy per molecule is twice as large at $600\,\text{K}$ as it is at $300\,\text{K}$.

    #### Check

    Kelvin temperature is proportional to average translational kinetic energy in this model, so doubling the kelvin temperature doubles that average energy.
  </WorkedExampleCard>
</ProblemSolvingSection>

# Energy

One of the most useful entry points into energy is the **work-energy theorem**. Rather than starting with a list of energy types, it starts with a direct statement about motion: when the net force does work on a system, the kinetic energy changes. From there, we can reinterpret the work done by conservative forces as a change in a potential-energy function and build the familiar conservation laws from that idea.

## The Work-Energy Theorem

For a moving object, the kinetic-energy term is

$$
K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2.
$$

Work measures energy transfer caused by a force acting through a displacement, and the net work done on a system changes its kinetic energy:

$$
W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K.
$$

This theorem is often the fastest way to connect forces and motion. If gravity, a spring, or friction does work on an object, the kinetic energy responds. That statement is more fundamental than the later bookkeeping language. The energy framework becomes especially powerful once we recognize that some kinds of work can be packaged into a new function.

## Reinterpreting Conservative Work as Potential Energy

Suppose a force is **conservative**, meaning the work it does depends only on the starting and ending points, not on the path taken. In that case, we can define a potential-energy function so that

$$
W_{\text{cons}} = -\Delta U.
$$

That lets us replace a work calculation with a bookkeeping statement about stored energy. Gravity is the standard example near Earth's surface:

$$
U_g = mgh.
$$

Now the work-energy theorem becomes

$$
\Delta K + \Delta U = W_{\text{non-cons}}.
$$

If only conservative forces act, then the non-conservative work term is zero and the combination $K + U$ stays fixed. In other words, the usual conservation law is not a separate mystery; it is what the work-energy theorem looks like after we reinterpret conservative work as a potential-energy change.

## Systems, Conservation, and Friction

Before writing a final energy equation, decide what belongs in the **system**. If the system is just a moving cart, then gravity and friction may look like outside influences. If the system includes the cart, the track, and Earth, then gravitational potential energy and thermal energy become part of the bookkeeping inside the system.

When friction is negligible, the cart continually trades gravitational potential energy and kinetic energy:

$$
E_{\text{mech}} = K + U_g \approx \text{constant}.
$$

When friction matters, the mechanical energy is no longer constant by itself. A more complete ledger includes the energy converted into thermal energy:

$$
E_{\text{total}} = K + U_g + E_{\text{thermal}}.
$$

That is why collisions can "lose" kinetic energy without violating conservation of energy, and why oscillations fade when damping is present. The energy is not destroyed; the work done by non-conservative forces is being recorded in a different column of the ledger.

As you explore the track below, try this sequence:

1. Set track friction to zero and release the cart from a high point. Watch the kinetic and gravitational bars trade places while the total stays fixed.
2. Keep the same release height but increase the mass. Notice that the energy values grow, yet the frictionless speed reached near the bottom stays the same.
3. Add track friction and compare the shrinking mechanical energy with the growing thermal-energy bar.
4. Change the gravity preset to compare how the same track shape behaves on the Moon, Earth, and Jupiter.

  >
    #### Problem

    A cart starts from rest $1.8\,\text{m}$ above the lowest point of a frictionless track. Find its speed at the bottom.

    #### Given

    - $h = 1.8\,\text{m}$
    - $g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$
    - starts from rest

    #### Use

    With no friction,

    $$
    mgh = \frac{1}{2}mv^2.
    $$

    The mass cancels, leaving

    $$
    v = \sqrt{2gh}.
    $$

    #### Solve

    $$
    v = \sqrt{2(9.8)(1.8)}
    = \sqrt{35.28}
    \approx 5.94\,\text{m/s}.
    $$

    #### Check

    The result depends only on the height drop, not on the cart mass. That is the same mass cancellation the explorer shows when you compare carts released from the same point.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard
    title="Include Thermal Energy from Friction"
    accent="red"
  >
    #### Problem

    A $2.0\,\text{kg}$ cart starts from rest and descends $1.5\,\text{m}$. Friction transfers $6.0\,\text{J}$ into thermal energy during the motion. Find the cart's final speed.

    #### Given

    - $m = 2.0\,\text{kg}$
    - $h = 1.5\,\text{m}$
    - $E_{\text{thermal}} = 6.0\,\text{J}$
    - starts from rest

    #### Use

    The lost gravitational potential energy is split between kinetic and thermal energy:

    $$
    mgh = K_f + E_{\text{thermal}}.
    $$

    #### Solve

    First find the available gravitational energy:

    $$
    mgh = (2.0)(9.8)(1.5) = 29.4\,\text{J}.
    $$

    So the final kinetic energy is

    $$
    K_f = 29.4 - 6.0 = 23.4\,\text{J}.
    $$

    Now solve for the speed:

    $$
    \frac{1}{2}(2.0)v^2 = 23.4
    \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad
    v^2 = 23.4
    \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad
    v \approx 4.84\,\text{m/s}.
    $$

    #### Check

    This speed is smaller than the frictionless value because part of the gravitational energy was redirected into thermal energy instead of staying in the mechanical ledger.
  </WorkedExampleCard>
</ProblemSolvingSection>

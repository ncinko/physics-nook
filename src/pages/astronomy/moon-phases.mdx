# Moon

The Moon does not change shape, and it makes no light of its own. Phases and eclipses are entirely a matter of **geometry** in the Sun-Earth-Moon system.

At any instant the Sun illuminates exactly one hemisphere of the Moon — the half facing the Sun. The phase we observe is simply **how much of that lit half can be seen from Earth**:

- **New moon** — the Moon sits between us and the Sun, so its lit side faces away. We see the dark side.
- **Full moon** — the Moon is opposite the Sun, so the entire lit side faces us.
- **Quarters** — the Moon is off to the side, and we see the lit half edge-on as a half disk.

We say the Moon is **waxing** when the lit fraction we see is growing from new toward full, and **waning** when it is shrinking from full back toward new.

## Lunar month

The Moon completes one orbit relative to the distant stars in the **sidereal month**, about $27.3$ days. But the cycle of phases — new moon to new moon — is the **synodic month**, about $29.5$ days. The phases take longer because Earth is also moving: while the Moon circles us, Earth advances along its own orbit, so the Sun's direction shifts and the Moon must travel a little *extra* each cycle to catch back up to the Sun–Earth line:

$$
\frac{1}{T_{\text{syn}}} = \frac{1}{T_{\text{sid}}} - \frac{1}{T_{\text{Earth}}}.
$$

This is the same "catching up" idea that explains retrograde motion in the next lesson — Earth's own motion hiding in the appearance.

We also only ever see one side of the Moon. That is not because it fails to spin — it is **tidally locked**, rotating exactly once per orbit. Earth's gravity raised a slight bulge on the Moon and, over billions of years, friction dragged its spin into step with its orbit, so the same hemisphere always faces home. In the explorer you can stand on the Moon and watch Earth hang nearly fixed in the sky to confirm it.

## Eclipses

If phases come purely from alignment, why isn't there a solar eclipse at every new moon and a lunar eclipse at every full moon? Because the Moon's orbit is tilted about $5^\circ$ relative to the plane of Earth's orbit (the ecliptic). Most months the Moon passes a little above or below the Sun–Earth line, and its shadow misses. Eclipses happen only when a new or full moon lands near a **node** — one of the two points where the tilted orbit crosses the ecliptic plane. That double requirement, right phase *and* near a node, is why eclipses come in seasons rather than monthly.

<ProblemSolvingSection title="Problem Solving">
  <WorkedExampleCard title="From Sidereal to Synodic Month" accent="blue">
    #### Problem

    The Moon's sidereal period is $T_{\text{sid}} = 27.3\,\text{days}$ and Earth's orbital period is $T_{\text{Earth}} = 365.25\,\text{days}$. Find the synodic month — the time from one new moon to the next.

    #### Use

    $$
    \frac{1}{T_{\text{syn}}} = \frac{1}{T_{\text{sid}}} - \frac{1}{T_{\text{Earth}}}.
    $$

    #### Solve

    $$
    \frac{1}{T_{\text{syn}}} = \frac{1}{27.3} - \frac{1}{365.25}
    \approx 0.03663 - 0.00274 = 0.03389\,\text{day}^{-1}.
    $$

    $$
    T_{\text{syn}} \approx \frac{1}{0.03389} \approx 29.5\,\text{days}.
    $$

    #### Check

    The synodic month is about $2.2$ days longer than the sidereal month — the extra time the Moon needs to catch up with the moving Sun–Earth line, and it matches the familiar length of the cycle of phases.

</ProblemSolvingSection>

# Entropy and the Second Law

The first law says energy is conserved. The second law asks a different question: which energy-conserving changes actually happen on their own?

A warm object can heat a cool object, but a cool object does not spontaneously get colder while making a warm object warmer. Gas released into a room spreads out, but room-temperature air does not spontaneously gather into one corner. The second law explains this one-way character using entropy.

## Macrostates and Microstates

A **macrostate** is a large-scale description: all particles in the left half, half on each side, a certain pressure, a certain total energy. A **microstate** is a detailed microscopic arrangement compatible with that description.

Many different microstates can share the same macrostate. For example, if $N$ particles can each be in the left or right half of a box, the macrostate with $n_L$ particles on the left has multiplicity

$$
\Omega(n_L) = \binom{N}{n_L}.
$$

The balanced macrostate usually has the largest multiplicity because there are many ways to choose which particles are on each side.

## Boltzmann Entropy

Boltzmann connected entropy to multiplicity:

$$
S = k_B \ln \Omega.
$$

The logarithm matters because it makes entropy additive for independent systems. If system A has $\Omega_A$ microstates and system B has $\Omega_B$ microstates, the combined system has $\Omega_A\Omega_B$ microstates, so

$$
S_{\text{total}} = k_B \ln(\Omega_A\Omega_B)
= k_B\ln\Omega_A + k_B\ln\Omega_B.
$$

Entropy is not a magic substance and not merely a synonym for messiness. It counts, in logarithmic form, how many microscopic arrangements fit the macroscopic facts we are tracking.

## The Second Law as Statistical Tendency

For an isolated system, the second law says entropy tends to increase:

$$
\Delta S_{\text{isolated}} \ge 0.
$$

The word "tends" is doing real work. Microscopic physics may allow a wildly unlikely decrease in entropy, but for macroscopic systems the number of higher-multiplicity states is so enormous that entropy-decreasing fluctuations are effectively never observed.

So the second law is not best remembered as "things get disordered." A sharper version is: isolated systems overwhelmingly evolve toward macrostates with larger multiplicity.

<ProblemSolvingSection>
  <WorkedExampleCard
    title="Entropy Change from a Multiplicity Ratio"
    accent="blue"
  >
    #### Problem

    A system changes from a macrostate with multiplicity $\Omega_i = 10^4$ to one with multiplicity $\Omega_f = 10^9$. Find the entropy change.

    #### Given

    - $\Omega_i = 10^4$
    - $\Omega_f = 10^9$

    #### Use

    $$
    \Delta S = k_B \ln\!\left(\frac{\Omega_f}{\Omega_i}\right).
    $$

    #### Solve

    $$
    \frac{\Omega_f}{\Omega_i} = \frac{10^9}{10^4} = 10^5.
    $$

    $$
    \Delta S = k_B \ln(10^5)
    = 5k_B\ln 10
    \approx 11.5k_B.
    $$

    #### Check

    The final macrostate has many more compatible microstates, so the entropy change is positive.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard
    title="Spontaneous or Unlikely?"
    accent="red"
  >
    #### Problem

    In an isolated box, $12$ gas particles start with all particles on the left side. They are then allowed to move between the left and right halves. Compare the multiplicity of the all-left macrostate with the six-left, six-right macrostate.

    #### Given

    - $N = 12$
    - all-left macrostate: $n_L = 12$
    - balanced macrostate: $n_L = 6$

    #### Use

    $$
    \Omega(n_L) = \binom{N}{n_L}.
    $$

    #### Solve

    $$
    \Omega(12) = \binom{12}{12} = 1.
    $$

    $$
    \Omega(6) = \binom{12}{6} = 924.
    $$

    The balanced macrostate has $924$ times as many compatible microstates.

    #### Check

    Even for only $12$ particles, the balanced arrangement is already much more likely. For macroscopic particle counts, the imbalance becomes overwhelmingly unlikely.
  </WorkedExampleCard>
</ProblemSolvingSection>

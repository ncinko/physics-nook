# Gauss’ Law

**Gauss’ law** connects the electric field on a closed boundary to the net charge inside it. If we imagine field lines flowing out of or into an imaginary box: the net flow depends only on the enclosed charge. 


Gauss’ law can be difficult to understand because it often (for an early physics student) requires two layers of abstraction away from what "feels intuitive":

- Rather than discussing the attractive/repulsive Coulomb interaction between charges, we are describing the effect of charges on an abstract **vector field** embedded in spacetime. 

- We are making statements about the structure of this field using imaginary surfaces. 


Before continuing, build intuition for the first layer of abstraction.  You should be comfortable representing the electric field as both a **vector field** (a vector giving the field at each point in space) and a **field-line sketch** (continuous curves whose tangents follow the field direction).  Ideally, you can also compute the electric field for a finite collection of point charges.

## Big Picture

A **Gaussian surface** is an imaginary closed boundary surrounding a volume of space, such as the surface of a sphere or all six faces of a box. It is purely a theoretical construction and has no effect on the electric field, charges, etc.

For now, read **electric flux** as a (signed) measure of how much field passes through a Gaussian surface. Although the electric field is not a flowing substance, like the water in a river, you may consider this analogy when applying Gauss' Law. The total flux considers outward-flowing contributions as positive, which are <span style={{ color: 'var(--accent-red)' }}>shaded in red</span> below. Likewise, inward-flowing flux is considered negative and <span style={{ color: 'var(--accent-blue)' }}>shaded in blue</span>.

To avoid clutter, the electric field vectors are only shown at points along the Gaussian surface.  The vector arrows only shown direction, but the magnitude can be inferred by the shading.

## Surface Normals

To measure how much field passes through a surface, we need its component perpendicular to that surface. A **normal vector** is an arrow perpendicular to a surface; a **unit normal**, $\hat{\mathbf n}$, has magnitude one and specifies its orientation. A flat surface has two possible normal directions. Choose one: field along that direction contributes positive flux, and field opposing it contributes negative flux.

For a flat surface in a uniform field,

$$
\Phi_E = EA\cos\theta,
$$

where $E$ is the field magnitude, $A$ is the area, $\theta$ is the angle between the field and the chosen normal, and $\Phi_E$ denotes electric flux. Flux has units of $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}$.

<InteractiveAnchor id="electric-flux-patch" />

<ElectricFluxPatch client:load />

When the normal points along the field, $\theta=0$ and $\Phi_E=EA$. When the field runs along the surface, $\theta=90^\circ$ and the flux is zero. Reversing the normal reverses the sign of the flux.

<DerivationPanel title="Why does the cosine appear?">

Only the perpendicular component $E\cos\theta$ contributes. Equivalently, the area presented to the field is the projected area $A\cos\theta$. Multiplying either way gives $\Phi_E=EA\cos\theta$.

We can package the area and orientation into an **area vector**, $\mathbf A=A\hat{\mathbf n}$. The same relation is the dot product

$$
\Phi_E=\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf A.
$$

If the field varies or the surface curves, divide the surface into tiny patches, each with its own normal, and add their contributions:

$$
\Phi_E\approx\sum_i \mathbf E_i\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}_i\,\Delta A_i
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\Phi_E=\int_S \mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf A.
$$

Here $\mathbf E_i$ is the field at patch $i$, $\hat{\mathbf n}_i$ is its unit normal, and $\Delta A_i$ is its area. The integral over the surface $S$ is the limit of that sum as the patches become small, with $d\mathbf A=\hat{\mathbf n}\,dA$ the infinitesimal area vector and $\mathbf E$ the field at that point.

</DerivationPanel>

## Closing the Surface

A **closed surface** surrounds a volume completely, like the six faces of a box or the surface of a sphere. For a closed surface, we choose every normal to point *outward*. A field pointing out contributes positive flux; a field pointing in contributes negative flux.

In a uniform field, a box has just as much inward flux as outward flux. Inspect its faces below, then change the field’s direction.

The net flux is zero even though the field is nonzero on the surface and throughout the box. Zero net flux means the signed contributions cancel; it does not mean there is no field.

## Charge Inside, Flux Outside

Now that we have a mathematical definition of electric flux, we can state Gauss’ law:

$$
\sum_\text{surface} (\mathbf E\cdot \mathbf{\hat{n}}) \Delta A = \oint_S \mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf A
=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{enclosed}}}{\varepsilon_0}.
$$

The sum is over the entire surface, chopped up into little pieces of area $\Delta A$.  Note that the electric field and normal vector can be different at every point along the surface.  More compactly, we write it as a closed surface integral. $Q_{\mathrm{enclosed}}$ is the *net* charge inside inside the surface, and $\varepsilon_0\approx8.85\times10^{-12}\ \text{C}^2/(\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2)$ is the **electric constant**. It is related to Coulomb’s constant by $k=1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$.

This is the precise version of the relationship you explored above. Return to the [explorer](#gauss-law-explorer), and switch on **outward normals** and **inspect patches**. The shading and patch readout describe the signed normal component $\mathbf E\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}$.  The corresponding flux of the patch is then scaled by its area. 

<DerivationPanel title="How does this agree with Coulomb’s law?">

Place a point charge $Q$ at the center of a sphere of radius $r$. Its field is radial, so the normal component everywhere on the sphere is $kQ/r^2$. The sphere has area $4\pi r^2$, giving

$$
\Phi_E=\frac{kQ}{r^2}\,4\pi r^2
=4\pi kQ
=\frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0}.
$$

The inverse-square decrease in the field exactly balances the increase in area. A larger sphere has the same total flux. The sign of $Q$ determines whether the net flux is outward or inward.

This calculation shows the spherical case. The full law applies to any closed surface: changing its shape redistributes the local contributions without changing their sum, as long as no charge crosses the boundary.

</DerivationPanel>

<DerivationPanel title="What about charges outside the surface?">

An external charge can produce a strong field on a Gaussian surface. Its contributions to the total flux nevertheless cancel: some point inward and others outward. The cancellation follows from the inverse-square field and the orientation and area of the surface patches; the field strengths at the entry and exit points need not be equal.

By superposition, every charge contributes to the field $\mathbf E$ on the left side of Gauss’ law. Only enclosed charges contribute to the net flux on the right side. Two equal and opposite enclosed charges also give zero net flux, despite producing a nonzero field.

</DerivationPanel>

## Using Symmetry

Gauss’ law is true for every closed surface.  It becomes a shortcut for finding $E$ when symmetry tells us the field’s direction and where its magnitude is constant. The trick is in identifying the symmetry of the charge distribution and then choosing a surface that matches this symmetry.

This isn't always possible.  But when it is: identify where the field is normal or tangent to the surface, simplify the flux integral, calculate the enclosed charge, and find the field.

<DerivationPanel title="Spherical symmetry: a charged sphere">

<GaussSymmetry kind="sphere" />

For a spherically symmetric charge distribution, choose a concentric Gaussian sphere. The field is radial and has constant magnitude on that sphere. For positive enclosed charge,

$$
E(4\pi r^2)=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{enclosed}}}{\varepsilon_0}.
$$

Outside a sphere of radius $R$ and total charge $Q$, all its charge is enclosed, so $E=kQ/r^2$.

Inside a *uniformly charged solid insulating sphere*, only the fraction $r^3/R^3$ of the charge is enclosed:

$$
Q_{\mathrm{enclosed}}=Q\frac{r^3}{R^3},
\qquad E=\frac{kQr}{R^3}\quad(r<R).
$$

The field rises linearly from zero at the center, then falls as $1/r^2$ outside. This differs from a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium, whose field inside the conducting material is zero.

</DerivationPanel>

<DerivationPanel title="Cylindrical symmetry: an infinite line of charge">

<GaussSymmetry kind="cylinder" />

For an infinite straight line with uniform charge per length $\lambda$, use a coaxial cylinder of radius $r$ and length $L$. The field is radial and constant on the curved side. It is tangent to both end caps, which contribute zero flux.

$$
E(2\pi rL)=\frac{\lambda L}{\varepsilon_0},
\qquad E=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0r}.
$$

For positive $\lambda$, the field points away from the line; for negative $\lambda$, it points toward it. A long finite wire approximates this result near its middle when $r$ is small compared with its length.

</DerivationPanel>

<ProblemSolvingSection title="Problem Solving">
  <WorkedExampleCard title="Net Charge and Total Flux" accent="blue">
    #### Problem

    A closed surface encloses $+3\,\text{nC}$ and $-1\,\text{nC}$. A $+5\,\text{nC}$ charge lies outside. What is the net electric flux?

    #### Use

    $\Phi_E=Q_{\mathrm{enclosed}}/\varepsilon_0$.

    #### Solve

    $$
    Q_{\mathrm{enclosed}}=(3-1)\,\text{nC}=2\times10^{-9}\,\text{C},
    \qquad\Phi_E=\frac{2\times10^{-9}}{8.85\times10^{-12}}
    \approx226\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}.
    $$

    #### Check

    Positive net enclosed charge gives positive outward flux; the external charge affects the field but adds no net flux.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard title="Inside a Uniformly Charged Sphere" accent="red">
    #### Problem

    A solid insulating sphere of radius $R=0.20\,\text{m}$ carries a uniformly distributed charge $Q=8\,\text{nC}$. Find the field at $r=0.10\,\text{m}$.

    #### Use

    $Q_{\mathrm{enclosed}}=Q(r/R)^3$ and $E=kQ_{\mathrm{enclosed}}/r^2$.

    #### Solve

    $$
    Q_{\mathrm{enclosed}}=8\,\text{nC}\left(\frac{0.10}{0.20}\right)^3=1\,\text{nC},
    \qquad E\approx\frac{(9\times10^9)(1\times10^{-9})}{(0.10)^2}
    =900\,\text{N/C},
    $$

    directed radially outward.

    #### Check

    At half the sphere’s radius, the field is half its surface value, consistent with the linear dependence inside.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard title="Field of an Infinite Sheet" accent="blue">
    #### Problem

    An isolated infinite sheet has uniform charge density $\sigma=4\,\text{nC/m}^2$. Find the field magnitude on either side.

    #### Use

    A pillbox has flux through two end caps: $2EA=\sigma A/\varepsilon_0$.

    #### Solve

    $$
    E=\frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}
    =\frac{4\times10^{-9}}{2(8.85\times10^{-12})}
    \approx226\,\text{N/C}.
    $$

    The field points away from the positively charged sheet on each side.

    #### Check

    The arbitrary cap area cancels, and both sides contribute equally to the outward flux.
  </WorkedExampleCard>
</ProblemSolvingSection>

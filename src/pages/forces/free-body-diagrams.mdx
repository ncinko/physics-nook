# Free-Body Diagrams

The [Forces](/forces) page introduced the cast: weight, spring forces, the normal force, and tension. This page is about using them. Before drawing a single arrow, it helps to notice something the cast list hides: a force is never a property of one object. Every force is one half of an **interaction** between two objects.

A falling ball is the simplest case. Earth pulls on the ball, and the ball pulls on Earth. We draw that as two objects joined by one link, labeled with the kind of interaction.

<InteractiveAnchor id="interaction-diagram" />

<InteractionDiagram client:visible />

## Interactions

An **interaction diagram** is a map of who is interacting with whom. Each object gets a bubble. Each interaction gets one link, labeled by its type: gravity, normal, tension, friction, or a push. There are no arrows yet, and no sizes. The only question is *which* objects interact, and *how*.

One link always stands for two forces: one acting on each object, pointing in opposite directions. This is **Newton's third law**:

$$
\vec{F}_{\htmlClass{math-hint math-hint-on}{\text{A on B}}} = -\vec{F}_{\text{B on A}}.
$$

<MathHint id="on" label="Subscripts">Read "A on B" as the force that object A exerts on object B. Swapping the names gives the partner force.</MathHint>

The two forces in a pair are the same kind of interaction, equal in size, opposite in direction, and they always act on **different objects**. That last fact is why a single link joins two *different* bubbles.

Switch to *Newt pushing a box* and find the friction link between Newt and Earth. Newt's feet push the floor backward, so the floor pushes Newt forward. That forward push from the floor is the only thing that can move Newt, and nothing about it breaks the third law.

## Choosing a System

An interaction diagram shows everything. A free-body diagram shows one piece of it. To pick the piece, draw a dashed **system boundary** around the object or objects you care about.

- A link that **crosses** the boundary is an external interaction. Its force on the object inside becomes an arrow on the free-body diagram.
- A link with **both ends inside** is internal. Both of its forces act on the system, they cancel, and neither goes on the diagram.

Tap the bubbles to move them in or out of the system.

<InteractiveAnchor id="system-boundary" />

<InteractionDiagram client:visible stage="system" initialScene="pushing-box" />

With only Newt inside, the box pushes him backward. Add the box to the system and that push vanishes from the diagram. Nothing physical changed; the push just became internal. Choosing a clever system is often the fastest way through a problem, because it hides the forces you do not want to solve for.

## Free-Body Diagrams

A **free-body diagram** shows the chosen system — often drawn as a single dot — with every external force acting on it as an arrow, and nothing else in the picture. Reading one off an interaction diagram means following three rules:

- **Only forces on the system.** The partner of each force acts on the object at the other end of the link, outside the boundary.
- **Only forces acting now.** The shove that started an object moving is not on the diagram once your hand is gone.
- **Only forces.** Velocity is not a force. Acceleration is not a force. The quantity $m\vec{a}$ is not a force — it is what the forces add up to.

Try each of these on Newt. Choose the forces you think belong, look at the diagram you just described, then check it.

The resting case is worth dwelling on. Newt pushes down on the table and the table pushes up on Newt: those two forces share the *normal* link, so they are a third-law pair, and only one of them acts on Newt. What balances Newt's weight is the normal force from the table — a force from a different link, whose partner lives on the table. The weight's own partner is Newt's pull on Earth, at the far end of the gravity link. If third-law pairs could cancel on one diagram, nothing would ever accelerate.

## Newton's First and Second Laws

**Newton's first law** says an object's velocity does not change unless a net force acts on it. This is the [force-free motion](/forces#force-free-newt) idea from the previous page: no net force means no acceleration, whether the object is sitting still or already moving.

**Newton's second law** says the net force sets the acceleration. Once the free-body diagram is drawn, the sum runs over its arrows, and nothing else:

<ComponentReveal>
  <div slot="vector">
    $$
    \sum \vec{F} = m\vec{a},
    $$
  </div>

  $$
  \begin{aligned}
  \textstyle\sum F_x &= ma_x,\\
  \textstyle\sum F_y &= ma_y.
  \end{aligned}
  $$
</ComponentReveal>

The first law is just the special case $\sum \vec{F} = 0$. As in [2D kinematics](/kinematics/two-dimensional), each component equation is independent, provided the axes are perpendicular and stay fixed for the whole problem.

The sum is a *vector* sum, so it is worth seeing drawn out. Newt stands in an elevator that is speeding up as it rises:

<ComponentReveal label="Diagram">
  <div slot="vector">
    $$
    \vec{N} + m\vec{g} = \sum \vec{F}
    $$
  </div>

  <NetForceDiagram />
</ComponentReveal>

The normal force has to be longer than the weight. If the two were equal, the arrows would close up with nothing left over, and Newt would not accelerate. The small leftover arrow is the net force, and it points the way Newt is accelerating: up. That is why the floor of a rising elevator feels like it presses harder on your feet.

<ProblemSolvingSection intro="Each of these follows the same path: map the interactions, draw a boundary around a system, then write the second law along each axis.">
  <WorkedExampleCard title="Apparent Weight in an Elevator" accent="blue">
    #### Problem

    A $60\ \text{kg}$ passenger stands on a scale in an elevator accelerating upward at $2.0\ \text{m/s}^2$. What does the scale read? Use $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.

    #### Use

    The system is the passenger. Two links cross the boundary: gravity with Earth and normal with the scale. Taking up as positive,

    $$
    \sum F_y = N - mg = ma_y.
    $$

    #### Solve

    Solving for the normal force,

    $$
    N = m(g + a_y) = (60)(9.8 + 2.0) = 708\ \text{N}.
    $$

    The scale reads the push it receives from the passenger, which is the third-law partner of $N$, so it reads $708\ \text{N}$.

    #### Check

    Larger than the $588\ \text{N}$ weight, as it must be: the net force has to point up to accelerate the passenger up.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard title="Pushing Two Boxes" accent="red">
    #### Problem

    On a frictionless floor, you push box A ($4.0\ \text{kg}$) with a horizontal force of $24\ \text{N}$. Box A pushes box B ($2.0\ \text{kg}$) ahead of it. Find the acceleration and the force box A exerts on box B.

    #### Use

    The interaction diagram has a *push* link between your hand and A, and another *push* link between A and B. Along the floor, nothing else acts. Two different boundaries answer the two questions:

    - Around **A and B together**, the A–B push is internal, so only your $24\ \text{N}$ crosses the boundary.
    - Around **B alone**, only the push from A crosses.

    #### Solve

    For the two-box system,

    $$
    a = \frac{F}{m_A + m_B} = \frac{24}{6.0} = 4.0\ \text{m/s}^2.
    $$

    For box B alone,

    $$
    F_{\text{A on B}} = m_B a = (2.0)(4.0) = 8.0\ \text{N}.
    $$

    #### Check

    On box A alone the forces are $24\ \text{N}$ forward and the $8.0\ \text{N}$ partner backward, and $16\ \text{N} = (4.0)(4.0)$, so both boxes agree on the same acceleration.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard title="Two Blocks and One Rope" accent="blue">
    #### Problem

    A $3.0\ \text{kg}$ block on a frictionless table is connected by a light rope over a pulley to a $2.0\ \text{kg}$ block hanging off the edge. Find the acceleration and the rope tension.

    #### Use

    Draw a boundary around *each* block. The table block has tension $T$ crossing its boundary horizontally; the hanging block has its weight $m_2g$ down and tension $T$ up. The rope links them, so both share one acceleration magnitude $a$:

    $$
    T = m_1a,
    \qquad
    m_2g - T = m_2a.
    $$

    #### Solve

    Adding the two equations eliminates $T$:

    $$
    m_2g = (m_1 + m_2)a
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    a = \frac{(2.0)(9.8)}{5.0} = 3.9\ \text{m/s}^2.
    $$

    Then $T = m_1a = (3.0)(3.9) = 11.8\ \text{N}$.

    #### Check

    Drawing one boundary around both blocks and the rope makes the tension internal and gives $m_2g = (m_1 + m_2)a$ in a single step, the same equation the addition produced.
  </WorkedExampleCard>
</ProblemSolvingSection>

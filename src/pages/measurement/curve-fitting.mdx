# Fitting Data

Suppose you charge a capacitor through a resistor and read its voltage with a voltmeter every half second. You end up with a list of numbers, but what you *want* is a single number: the **time constant** $\tau = RC$, which says how quickly the capacitor charges. A **fit** is how you get from one to the other. You choose a **model** (an equation with adjustable **parameters**), then find the parameter values that bring the curve closest to the data.

"Closest" is made precise with **residuals**: the residual of a data point is the measured value minus the model's value at that same time,

$$
r_i = V_i - V_\text{model}(t_i).
$$

A good fit leaves small residuals. A *useful* fit also leaves residuals with no pattern, and that second requirement is what the rest of this page leans on.

## A line is the simplest model

The first model most of us reach for is a straight line, $V = a + bt$. Fitting a line has real advantages: the best $a$ and $b$ come from a formula, no guessing required, and the uncertainties in $a$ and $b$ do too. For data that really are linear, such as distance against time at constant speed, nothing more is needed.

Try it on the capacitor data. Everything here comes from one experiment: twenty voltmeter readings, each uncertain by $\pm 0.1\ \text{V}$.

The line passes through the middle of the cloud and the software reports confident-looking values of $a$ and $b$, but the residuals arch from negative to positive and back. Every point isn't far off, but the *errors lean the same way together*. That is the signature of a wrong model. Charging slows down as the capacitor fills; a line cannot slow down.

## A functional fit

Physics tells us what shape to expect. A capacitor charging toward the battery voltage follows

$$
V(t) = V_0\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right),
$$

which has two parameters with physical meaning: $V_0$, the voltage the capacitor is heading toward, and $\tau$, the time it takes to cover about $63\%$ of the remaining distance. Switch the plot above to the exponential and the residuals lose their arch, scattering about zero mostly inside the $\pm 0.1\ \text{V}$ band.

There is no formula that hands you the best $V_0$ and $\tau$. Instead the computer (or you, in Desmos) adjusts them iteratively until the curve can't be improved. The idea is the same as the line fit's, with one extra step: guess, compare to the data, adjust, repeat.

It is tempting to dodge this by *straightening* the data. Since $V_0 - V = V_0 e^{-t/\tau}$, a graph of $\ln(V_0 - V)$ against $t$ is a straight line with slope $-1/\tau$. This works, but poorly. You need $V_0$ before you can plot anything, even though it is one of the things you are trying to fit. Late in the charge, $V_0 - V$ is tiny, so the same voltmeter noise becomes a huge relative error (and can even make the logarithm undefined). Taking the log also distorts the error bars, so a plain line fit gives those noisy late points the same say as the good early ones. Fitting the original curve avoids all of this.

## Reading residuals

Whatever the model, plot the residuals against $t$ and ask whether they look like **noise**:

- **Random scatter about zero**, with a size comparable to the error bars: the model is doing its job.
- **A systematic pattern** (an arch, a trend, a run of points all on one side): the model is wrong, or the parameters are not at their best values.

That second line is the key to estimating uncertainty.

## Uncertainty by hand

Your best-fit $\tau$ is not a perfect number. Slightly different values of $\tau$ would have described the data almost as well. The uncertainty in $\tau$ is the range of values that still describe the data acceptably, and you can find it directly: leave the best fit, increase $\tau$ until the residuals clearly develop a pattern, and note where that happened. Then do the same in the other direction. Half the distance between the two edges is the uncertainty.

One wrinkle: $V_0$ and $\tau$ are linked. A larger $\tau$ makes the curve rise more slowly, which can be partly compensated by a larger $V_0$. If you move $\tau$ and leave $V_0$ alone, the fit will look worse than it needs to and you will underestimate how much $\tau$ can wander. So each time you move $\tau$, **re-adjust $V_0$ to its best value** before judging the residuals. Turn on "Re-adjust $V_0$ for me" to see what a fully re-tuned fit looks like.

## A first look at $\chi^2$

Judging "clearly develops a pattern" by eye works, but it is subjective, and different people mark different edges. The **chi-square** statistic gives the same judgment a number. For each point, measure the residual in units of that point's own uncertainty $\sigma_i$, square it so that positive and negative misses both count, and add them up:

$$
\chi^2 = \sum_i \left(\frac{V_i - V_\text{model}(t_i)}{\sigma_i}\right)^2.
$$

A point that misses by about one $\sigma$ adds about $1$, so a good fit to $N$ points with $p$ parameters has $\chi^2 \approx N - p$. The best-fit parameters are the ones that make $\chi^2$ as small as possible, and the uncertainty has a clean rule: **the uncertainty in a parameter is how far you can move it, re-optimizing the others, before $\chi^2$ rises by $1$ above its minimum.** That is your by-hand procedure with an exact stopping point. Reveal the answer in the plot above and compare it with your own marks.

We will use $\chi^2$ more carefully later. For now, notice that nothing new is being measured. The residuals and error bars are the same ones you have been reading by eye.

## Other ways to get an uncertainty

Fitting software reports an uncertainty for every parameter, and for good fits it agrees with the $\chi^2$ rule. But there is a more direct, assumption-free check: **do the experiment again.** Each run has its own voltmeter noise, so each run yields a slightly different $\tau$. The spread of those values is the uncertainty, measured directly.

<InteractiveAnchor id="repeat-trials" />

<RepeatTrials client:visible />

The scatter across many runs matches the uncertainty from fitting a single run. In the lab, three or four repeats are all you can usually afford, so the single-run methods carry most of the weight. But a repeat is the best test of whether you believe them.

Finally, a fitted $\tau$ is something you can compare with theory, exactly as you compared your value of $\pi$ in [Uncertainty](/measurement/uncertainty). Here, theory is $\tau = RC$ from the measured resistor and capacitor, where the physics of both is on the [Capacitance](/electromagnetism/capacitance) page, and a [circuit simulation](/electromagnetism/current#circuit-kit) lets you watch the same charging curve form.

  <WorkedExampleCard
    title="Reporting the time constant"
    accent="blue"
  >
    #### Problem

    Fitting $V_0\left(1-e^{-t/\tau}\right)$ to a charging curve and re-adjusting $V_0$ at each step, you find the residuals still look random for $\tau$ from $2.10\ \text{s}$ to $2.24\ \text{s}$. Report $\tau$.

    #### Use

    The best estimate is the middle of the acceptable range and the uncertainty is its half-width.

    #### Solve

    $$
    \tau = \frac{2.10 + 2.24}{2} = 2.17\ \text{s}, \qquad \delta\tau = \frac{2.24 - 2.10}{2} = 0.07\ \text{s}.
    $$

    So $\tau = 2.17 \pm 0.07\ \text{s}$.

    #### Check

    The uncertainty is rounded to one significant figure and the value to the same decimal place, as before. An uncertainty of $0.07\ \text{s}$ is about $3\%$ of $\tau$, plausible for twenty points with $\pm 0.1\ \text{V}$ error bars.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard
    title="Does the fit agree with RC?"
    accent="red"
  >
    #### Problem

    The circuit used $R = 100 \pm 5\ \text{k}\Omega$ and $C = 22 \pm 2\ \mu\text{F}$. Does the fitted $\tau = 2.17 \pm 0.07\ \text{s}$ agree with $\tau = RC$?

    #### Use

    $RC$ is a product, so relative uncertainties add. Then check whether the fitted range overlaps the predicted one.

    #### Solve

    $$
    RC = (100\ \text{k}\Omega)(22\ \mu\text{F}) = 2.2\ \text{s}, \qquad
    \frac{\delta(RC)}{RC} = \frac{5}{100} + \frac{2}{22} = 5.0\% + 9.1\% = 14\%,
    $$

    so $RC = 2.2 \pm 0.3\ \text{s}$. The two ranges, $[2.10,\ 2.24]$ and $[1.9,\ 2.5]$, overlap, and the discrepancy is $\tfrac{|2.17 - 2.2|}{0.3} \approx 0.1$ uncertainties.

    #### Verdict

    **Consistent** — but notice which uncertainty is doing the limiting. The fit pins $\tau$ down about four times better than the component tolerances do, so this test can only be as sharp as the parts you used. Measuring $R$ and $C$ more carefully, rather than collecting more voltage readings, is what would sharpen the comparison.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

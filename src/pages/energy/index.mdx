# Energy

Energy is one of the most useful ideas in physics because it is a single number that we can track as a system changes. In mechanics we will meet it in two forms first: the energy an object has **because it is moving**, and the energy it has **because of where it sits in a gravitational field**. This page introduces both, then lets you watch one turn into the other by picking up a ball and dropping it.

## Kinetic Energy

**Kinetic energy** is the energy of motion. For an object of mass $m$ moving with speed $v$, it is defined as

$$
K = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2.
$$

Two features of this definition are worth pausing on. First, kinetic energy grows with mass: a heavier object moving at the same speed carries more of it. Second, and more importantly, it depends on the **square** of the speed. Doubling the speed does not double the kinetic energy — it quadruples it. Kinetic energy is measured in **joules** (J), where $1\,\text{J} = 1\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$.

## Gravitational Potential Energy

**Gravitational potential energy** is energy stored by virtue of an object's height. Near Earth's surface, where the gravitational field strength $g \approx 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$ is essentially constant, it is defined as

$$
U = mgh,
$$

where $h$ is the height of the object above a reference level we are free to choose. Lifting a ball higher stores more potential energy; letting it down spends that store. Because only *changes* in height matter, the choice of where $h = 0$ is just bookkeeping — here we will measure height from the ground.

Potential energy is also measured in joules, which is what makes the next step possible: because $K$ and $U$ are measured in the same units, we can add them and watch the **total** as an object moves.

**Reading the stacked bar chart.** This is the kind of diagram we draw constantly in class, so it is worth learning to read. Each colored block is one form of energy, drawn to scale: the purple block is gravitational potential energy, the green block is kinetic energy, and they are *stacked* so the height of the whole column is the **total** energy. Watch what happens as the ball falls:

- At the top, the column is entirely purple — all potential, no kinetic.
- As it descends, the purple block shrinks while the green block grows by exactly the same amount.
- Just before it lands, the column is almost entirely green — nearly all kinetic.
- The **top of the stack never moves.** The dashed "total" line stays put, because the energy is only changing form, not appearing or disappearing.

Try sliding the **mass** higher: both blocks get taller, because $K$ and $U$ each scale with $m$ — yet the ball still reaches the ground at the same speed, since that speed depends on the height dropped, not the mass.

## Worked Examples

<ProblemSolvingSection>
  <WorkedExampleCard
    title="Speed of a Dropped Ball"
    accent="blue"
  >
    #### Problem

    A $2.0\,\text{kg}$ ball is held at rest $3.0\,\text{m}$ above the ground and released. How fast is it moving just before it lands?

    #### Given

    - $m = 2.0\,\text{kg}$
    - $h = 3.0\,\text{m}$
    - released from rest, so $K_{\text{top}} = 0$

    #### Use

    At the top, all of the energy is potential:

    $$
    U_{\text{top}} = mgh = (2.0)(9.8)(3.0) = 58.8\,\text{J}.
    $$

    By the time it reaches the ground, all of that has become kinetic energy, so $K_{\text{bottom}} = 58.8\,\text{J}$. Setting $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = K_{\text{bottom}}$ and solving for the speed:

    $$
    v = \sqrt{\frac{2K_{\text{bottom}}}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(58.8)}{2.0}}.
    $$

    #### Solve

    $$
    v = \sqrt{58.8} \approx 7.7\,\text{m/s}.
    $$

    #### Check

    Notice the mass cancels: $v = \sqrt{2gh}$. Two balls of different mass dropped from the same height land at the same speed — exactly what the explorer shows when you change the mass slider.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard
    title="Reading the Bar Chart Partway Down"
    accent="red"
  >
    #### Problem

    A $1.5\,\text{kg}$ ball is dropped from $4.0\,\text{m}$. When it has fallen to a height of $1.0\,\text{m}$, how much energy is kinetic and how much is potential?

    #### Given

    - $m = 1.5\,\text{kg}$
    - release height $h_0 = 4.0\,\text{m}$, current height $h = 1.0\,\text{m}$

    #### Use

    The total energy equals the potential energy at the release point:

    $$
    E = mgh_0 = (1.5)(9.8)(4.0) = 58.8\,\text{J}.
    $$

    The potential energy now is

    $$
    U = mgh = (1.5)(9.8)(1.0) = 14.7\,\text{J},
    $$

    and the rest of the total must be kinetic.

    #### Solve

    $$
    K = E - U = 58.8 - 14.7 = 44.1\,\text{J}.
    $$

    #### Check

    On a stacked bar chart, draw a purple block of $14.7\,\text{J}$ with a green block of $44.1\,\text{J}$ on top — together they reach the same $58.8\,\text{J}$ total line as at the start.
  </WorkedExampleCard>
</ProblemSolvingSection>

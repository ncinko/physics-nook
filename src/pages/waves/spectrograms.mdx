# Spectrograms

A **spectrogram** is a plot that allows us to visualize the time-dependent frequency content of sound.  It shows which frequencies are present, how strong each one is, and how these change over time.

To build one, the sound is chopped into short slices (windows) and the frequency content of each slice is found through a Fourier transform. Each frequency spectrum is mapped onto a column of colors (encoding the "strength" of each frequency), with low frequencies at the bottom and high ones at the top. These columns then scroll past as time progresses.

Altogether, we are left with an animated graph:

- the horizontal axis is **time**, with the present moment at the right edge
- the vertical axis is **frequency**, low at the bottom
- color is **level**, how strong that frequency was at that moment

Everything below happens inside your browser. No audio is uploaded, stored, or recorded.

There are many patterns to be found in natural (and synthesized) sounds.  A steady horizontal line is a constant tone with one frequency.  A collection of evenly spaced lines is known as a **harmonic stack**.  A vertical streak is a click, handclap, or some other percussive sound. 

Note that many instruments, including your voice, do not produce a single frequency.  They often produce a harmonic stack: a fundamental frequency $f_1$ together with **harmonics** at integer multiples $2f_1, 3f_1, 4f_1, \ldots$.  A first-year physics course will usually explain the mechanism by which these harmonics are produced.

The quietest sound you can hear and a sound loud enough to hurt differ in amplitude by a factor of around a million. With a linear "loudness scale", it is difficult to visually distinguish between quiet and loud.  So the scale is logarithmic, measured in **decibels**:

$$
L = 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{A}{A_0}\right).
$$

Every $20\ \text{dB}$ is a factor of ten in amplitude.  In the spectrogram, the Floor control sets where the color scale bottoms out: raise it to remove background noise, leaving only the strong components.

Likewise, the vertical frequency axis is also logarithmic.  Equal frequency *ratios* take equal space, so every octave is the same height and a musical interval becomes a fixed distance no matter where on the display it sits.

## The Gabor Limit

The spectrogram does not consider the entire data set at once. It looks at $N$ samples (the window) at a time, taken at a sample rate $f_s$. That window lasts

$$
\Delta t = \frac{N}{f_s},
$$

and it cannot distinguish two frequencies closer together than

$$
\Delta f = \frac{f_s}{N}.
$$

Multiply them and the $N$ and $f_s$ both cancel:

$$
\Delta f\,\Delta t = 1.
$$

A short window pins down *when* something happened at the cost of frequency resolution. A long window better resolves the frequency content but loses temporal resolution. 

This is the **Gabor limit**, and it is not a limitation of this particular analyzer or of computers in general. It is a theorem about signal processing, and it is the same mathematics that reappears later as the uncertainty principle in quantum mechanics. 

<ProblemSolvingSection>
  <WorkedExampleCard title="Reading a Harmonic Stack" accent="blue">
    #### Problem

    A frozen spectrogram shows bright horizontal lines at $165\ \text{Hz}$, $330\ \text{Hz}$, $495\ \text{Hz}$, and $660\ \text{Hz}$, and nothing below $165\ \text{Hz}$. What note is sounding?

    #### Use

    Harmonics sit at integer multiples of the fundamental,

    $$
    f_n = n f_1,
    $$

    so the spacing between neighbouring lines is itself the fundamental.

    #### Solve

    The gaps are $330 - 165 = 165$, $495 - 330 = 165$, and $660 - 495 = 165\ \text{Hz}$. So $f_1 = 165\ \text{Hz}$, and the four lines are $f_1$ through $4f_1$. Converting to a note,

    $$
    n = 69 + 12\log_2\!\left(\frac{165}{440}\right) \approx 52,
    $$

    which is E3.

    #### Check

    The lines are equally spaced in hertz but not in ratio, so on the log axis they crowd together going up — the signature of one instrument rather than four unrelated tones.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard title="Choosing a Window" accent="red">
    #### Problem

    Two tones $4\ \text{Hz}$ apart are playing, slow enough that you hear them beat. The sample rate is $f_s = 48\,000\ \text{Hz}$. Will a $2048$-point window show them as two separate lines?

    #### Use

    $$
    \Delta f = \frac{f_s}{N}, \qquad \Delta t = \frac{N}{f_s}.
    $$

    #### Solve

    $$
    \Delta f = \frac{48\,000}{2048} \approx 23.4\ \text{Hz},
    $$

    which is far coarser than the $4\ \text{Hz}$ gap, so both tones land in the same resolution cell and paint a single thick line that pulses. Separating them needs

    $$
    N \gtrsim \frac{f_s}{\Delta f} = \frac{48\,000}{4} = 12\,000
    $$

    samples, so even the $8192$-point window is not quite enough — and it already spans $8192/48\,000 \approx 0.17\ \text{s}$.

    #### Check

    The pulsing is the beat itself, showing up on the time axis precisely because the frequency axis could not hold it.
  </WorkedExampleCard>
</ProblemSolvingSection>

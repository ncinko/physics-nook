# Quantization and Tunneling

Once a quantum system is described by a wavefunction, the next question is: which wavefunctions are actually allowed? The answer comes from the Schrödinger equation together with the boundary conditions imposed by the physical setup. Those two ingredients explain both **quantized bound states** and the seemingly strange effect of **tunneling**.

## The Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation

For a particle in one dimension, the time-independent Schrödinger equation is

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2 \psi}{dx^2} + V(x)\psi = E\psi.
$$

This equation plays a role somewhat analogous to Newton's second law in classical mechanics: it connects the system's environment, represented by the potential energy function $V(x)$, to the allowed states $\psi$ and their energies $E$.

## Boundary Conditions and Discrete Energies

Inside a rigid one-dimensional box, the walls force the wavefunction to vanish at the boundaries:

$$
\psi(0) = 0,
\qquad
\psi(L) = 0.
$$

Those conditions mean only standing waves that fit exactly inside the box are allowed. The result is a discrete family of states labeled by an integer $n$, with energies

$$
E_n = \frac{n^2 h^2}{8mL^2},
\qquad
n = 1, 2, 3, \dots
$$

The allowed energies are therefore **quantized**, not continuous. There is no $n=0$ bound state in this model, so even the lowest state carries nonzero energy.

## Why Tunneling Happens

A classical particle with energy less than a barrier height would simply bounce back. A quantum wavefunction behaves differently. In a barrier region where $E < V$, the wavefunction usually decays rather than dropping instantly to zero. If the barrier is thin enough, some amplitude survives to the far side. The transmitted intensity can be small, but it is not always zero.

That is the essence of **tunneling**. It does not mean the particle borrows energy in a careless way. It means the wavefunction satisfies the Schrödinger equation across the whole region, including the barrier, and the resulting probability for transmission can be nonzero.

## A Useful Scaling Idea

Even when you do not plug in constants, the structure of the formulas is revealing:

- For a fixed box width, energies grow like $n^2$.
- For a fixed quantum number, energies shrink like $1/L^2$.
- For tunneling, thicker or taller barriers reduce transmission very quickly because the wavefunction decays exponentially inside the forbidden region.

Those scaling rules are often more important for intuition than any single number.

<ProblemSolvingSection>
  <WorkedExampleCard
    title="Compare Particle-In-A-Box Energy Levels"
    accent="blue"
  >
    #### Problem

    In a one-dimensional infinite square well, compare the energies of the first three states by finding $E_2/E_1$ and $E_3/E_1$.

    #### Given

    $$
    E_n = \frac{n^2 h^2}{8mL^2}.
    $$

    #### Use

    Since the prefactor is the same for every level in the same box, the ratios depend only on $n^2$.

    #### Solve

    $$
    \frac{E_2}{E_1} = \frac{2^2}{1^2} = 4,
    \qquad
    \frac{E_3}{E_1} = \frac{3^2}{1^2} = 9.
    $$

    So the second state has four times the ground-state energy, and the third state has nine times the ground-state energy.

    #### Check

    The rapid growth reflects the fact that fitting shorter standing wavelengths into the same box requires stronger curvature in the wavefunction, which the Schrödinger equation associates with higher energy.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard
    title="See How A Wider Barrier Changes Transmission"
    accent="red"
  >
    #### Problem

    A simple tunneling estimate has the form

    $$
    T \approx e^{-2\kappa a},
    $$

    where $a$ is the barrier width. If the width doubles from $a$ to $2a$ while $\kappa$ stays the same, how does the transmission change?

    #### Given

    - initial transmission: $T_1 \approx e^{-2\kappa a}$
    - doubled width: $2a$

    #### Use

    Substitute the new width into the same exponential form.

    #### Solve

    $$
    T_2 \approx e^{-2\kappa (2a)} = e^{-4\kappa a}.
    $$

    But

    $$
    e^{-4\kappa a} = \left(e^{-2\kappa a}\right)^2.
    $$

    So

    $$
    T_2 \approx T_1^2.
    $$

    #### Check

    This shows why tunneling is so sensitive to barrier width: doubling the width can reduce the transmission dramatically rather than by a small linear factor.
  </WorkedExampleCard>
</ProblemSolvingSection>

# Electric Field

An **electric field** is the force per unit charge at a point in space. We define it by

$$
\vec{\mathbf E} = \frac{\vec{\mathbf F}}{q_{\text{test}}},
$$

where $\vec{\mathbf F}$ is the electric force acting on a small, positive **test charge** $q_{\text{test}}$. In other words, the field describes the force a test charge *would* feel if placed at a particular location.

If vector components and unit vectors feel rusty, the [Vectors](/math/vectors) page is a good warm-up — the field is a vector at every point in space.

## Point Charge

In the presence of a single point charge, the electric field is

$$
\vec{\mathbf E} = k\,\frac{q}{r^2}\,\hat{\mathbf r},
$$

where $k$ is Coulomb's constant and $q$ is the charge in coulombs.

<DerivationPanel title="Do these two definitions of the electric field agree with Coulomb's law?">

Start from Coulomb's law for the force on a test charge in the presence of a point charge:

$$
\vec{\mathbf F} = k\,\frac{q\,q_{\text{test}}}{r^2}\,\hat{\mathbf r}.
$$

Divide both sides by the test charge to get the electric field:

$$
\vec{\mathbf E} = \frac{\vec{\mathbf F}}{q_{\text{test}}} = k\,\frac{q}{r^2}\,\hat{\mathbf r}.
$$

</DerivationPanel>

The vector $\vec{\mathbf r}$ points from the charge's position to the location of interest where we are computing the field, so its magnitude $r = \lVert \vec{\mathbf r} \rVert$ is the distance from the charge. The direction of $\vec{\mathbf E}$ comes from the unit vector $\hat{\mathbf r} = \dfrac{\vec{\mathbf r}}{\lVert \vec{\mathbf r} \rVert}$.

## Superposition

When multiple charges are present, the net electric field is the vector sum of the individual fields:

$$
\vec{\mathbf E}_{\text{net}} = \sum_i \vec{\mathbf E}_i.
$$

With two point charges, the net field is simply $\vec{\mathbf E}_{\text{net}} = \vec{\mathbf E}_1 + \vec{\mathbf E}_2$, added tip-to-tail.

<DerivationPanel title="Is there one electric field, or many?">

We often speak of *individual* electric fields due to each point charge. This is a useful mental model for connecting to Coulomb's law and applying superposition.

In the long run, it is better to imagine that space is filled with a single, unified field $\vec{\mathbf E}_{\text{net}}$. The field is a property of space itself, shaped by the configuration of charges. The field then determines the force on charges within that space, which predicts their motion, which changes the field, and so on:

## Electric Force and Field Lines

The whole point of the field is that, once we know it, the net force on any charge placed in it is

$$
\vec{\mathbf F}_{\text{net}} = q_{\text{test}}\,\vec{\mathbf E}_{\text{net}}.
$$

<DerivationPanel title="Did we add any new physics?">

Not yet. So far the electric field is just a way to organize our thinking about forces between charges. Notice that the field is implicitly a function of position (and time), since the force on a test charge depends on where it sits:

$$
\vec{\mathbf E} = \vec{\mathbf E}(\vec{\mathbf r}, t).
$$

In these simulations the field updates *instantaneously* as charges move. That is not how reality works — the field concept becomes far more powerful in genuinely time-dependent situations, where changing electric fields generate magnetic fields and vice versa. That is the road to electromagnetism.

</DerivationPanel>



<ProblemSolvingSection title="Problem Solving">
  <WorkedExampleCard title="Field of a Point Charge" accent="blue">
    #### Problem

    Find the magnitude of the electric field $0.5\,\text{m}$ from a $2\,\mu\text{C}$ point charge.

    #### Given

    - $k \approx 9 \times 10^9\,\text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2$
    - $q = 2 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{C}$
    - $r = 0.5\,\text{m}$

    #### Solve

    $$
    E = k\,\frac{|q|}{r^2} = 9 \times 10^9 \cdot \frac{2 \times 10^{-6}}{(0.5)^2} \approx 7.2 \times 10^4\,\text{N/C}.
    $$
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard title="Net Field at a Midpoint" accent="blue">
    #### Problem

    Two identical $1\,\mu\text{C}$ point charges are placed $1\,\text{m}$ apart. Determine the net electric field at the midpoint between them.

    #### Solve

    At the midpoint, each charge contributes a field of equal magnitude pointing in the *opposite* direction (both fields point away from their like-signed source). The two contributions cancel:

    $$
    \vec{\mathbf E}_{\text{net}} = \vec{\mathbf E}_1 + \vec{\mathbf E}_2 = 0.
    $$
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard title="Why E = 0 Inside a Conductor" accent="red">
    #### Problem

    In electrostatic equilibrium (no motion of free charges), why must the electric field be zero inside a conductor?

    #### Reasoning

    If the field were nonzero, the free charges inside would feel a force $\vec{\mathbf F} = q\vec{\mathbf E}$, and a net force means acceleration — not equilibrium. The charges keep rearranging until their own field exactly cancels the interior field everywhere, at which point the motion stops. That equilibrium configuration is precisely the one with $\vec{\mathbf E} = 0$ inside.
  </WorkedExampleCard>
</ProblemSolvingSection>

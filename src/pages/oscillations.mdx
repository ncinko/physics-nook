# Oscillations

An oscillation is motion that repeats around an equilibrium point. Pull a mass on a spring, pluck a string, or nudge a pendulum and the system does not simply return once and stop. Inertia carries it past equilibrium, the restoring force pulls it back, and the cycle repeats. The cleanest version of that story is **simple harmonic motion**, where the restoring force is proportional to displacement.

## Equilibrium and Restoring Force

A stable equilibrium is a point the system naturally returns to after a small disturbance. For a spring, Hooke's law says

$$
F = -kx.
$$

In this case, $x=0$ is the **equilibrium point** of the system.  
If the mass is displaced by $x$, the spring pulls back with a force proportional to how far it has been stretched or compressed.  The minus sign matters: it reminds us that the force points back toward equilibrium. This type of force is called a **restoring force**.

  ## Period and Frequency

  The period $T$ is the interval of time it takes to complete one full oscillation, or cycle, of the system.  For an ideal mass-spring oscillator,

  $$
  T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}.
  $$

  A stiffer spring makes the oscillation quicker, while a larger mass makes it slower.  For small swings, a pendulum follows the same basic timing idea with

  $$
  T_{\text{pendulum}} \approx 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{L}{g}},
  $$

  so a longer pendulum swings more slowly than a shorter one. It's also convenient to define **frequency** $f$ and **angular frequency** $\omega$ as
  $$
  f = \frac{1}{T},
  \qquad
  \omega = \frac{2\pi}{T}.
  $$
  These measure cycles per second and radians per second, respectively (where each cycle corresponds to $2\pi$ radians).
  Note that "cycles" and "radians" are considered dimensionless.

Due to the restoring force, the acceleration is proportional to displacement and directed back toward equilibrium:

$$
a(t) = -\omega^2 x(t).
$$

## Energy Exchange

An oscillator is a continual trade between kinetic energy and stored potential energy. For a spring,

$$
U_s = \frac{1}{2}kx^2,
\qquad
K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2,
\qquad
E_{\text{mech}} = K + U_s.
$$

If damping is negligible, the total mechanical energy stays fixed and simply shifts form over the cycle. Real systems lose some of that mechanical energy to internal friction, air resistance, or sound, so the motion gradually fades unless something keeps driving it.

  <WorkedExampleCard
    title="Find the Period and Frequency of a Spring"
    accent="blue"
  >
    #### Problem

    A mass-spring oscillator has mass $m = 0.50\,\text{kg}$ and spring constant $k = 200\,\text{N/m}$. Find the period $T$ and frequency $f$.

    #### Given

    - $m = 0.50\,\text{kg}$
    - $k = 200\,\text{N/m}$

    #### Use

    For an ideal mass-spring oscillator,

    $$
    T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{m}{k}},
    \qquad
    f = \frac{1}{T}.
    $$

    #### Solve

    First compute the period:

    $$
    T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{0.50}{200}}
    = 2\pi \sqrt{0.0025}
    = 2\pi(0.05)
    \approx 0.314\,\text{s}.
    $$

    Now compute the frequency:

    $$
    f = \frac{1}{0.314} \approx 3.18\,\text{Hz}.
    $$

    #### Check

    The result makes sense: a fairly stiff spring and a modest mass should oscillate quickly, so a short period and a frequency a little above $3\,\text{Hz}$ are both reasonable.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard
    title="Use Energy to Find the Maximum Speed"
    accent="red"
  >
    #### Problem

    A spring with constant $k = 80\,\text{N/m}$ oscillates with amplitude $A = 0.15\,\text{m}$ while attached to a mass $m = 0.40\,\text{kg}$. Find the total mechanical energy and the maximum speed.

    #### Given

    - $k = 80\,\text{N/m}$
    - $A = 0.15\,\text{m}$
    - $m = 0.40\,\text{kg}$

    #### Use

    At the turning point, all of the mechanical energy is spring potential energy:

    $$
    E_{\text{mech}} = \frac{1}{2}kA^2.
    $$

    At equilibrium, the speed is largest and all of the mechanical energy is kinetic:

    $$
    E_{\text{mech}} = \frac{1}{2}mv_{\max}^2.
    $$

    #### Solve

    First find the total mechanical energy:

    $$
    E_{\text{mech}} = \frac{1}{2}(80)(0.15^2)
    = 40(0.0225)
    = 0.90\,\text{J}.
    $$

    Now set that equal to the maximum kinetic energy:

    $$
    0.90 = \frac{1}{2}(0.40)v_{\max}^2
    = 0.20v_{\max}^2.
    $$

    So

    $$
    v_{\max}^2 = \frac{0.90}{0.20} = 4.5
    \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad
    v_{\max} \approx 2.12\,\text{m/s}.
    $$

    #### Check

    The energy stays the same the whole time, but its form changes. At the turning points, the oscillator stores the full $0.90\,\text{J}$ as spring potential energy. At equilibrium, that same $0.90\,\text{J}$ appears as kinetic energy, which is why the speed is largest there.
  </WorkedExampleCard>
</ProblemSolvingSection>

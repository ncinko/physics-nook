# Free-Body Diagrams

The [Forces](/forces) page introduced many of the forces commonly seen in mechanics: gravity, the normal force, spring-like forces, etc.  Although we focused on Newt, a force is not a property of one object. Each force results from an **interaction** between two things.

An **interaction diagram** is a way of visualizing the interactions between several objects. Each object gets a bubble, which are then joined by lines that represent the various interactions.  The only question is *which* objects interact, and *how*.

<InteractiveAnchor id="interaction-diagram" />

<InteractionDiagram client:visible stage="pairs" />

Note that one interaction link always represents two forces. Tap a link above to see them: one force acts on each object, and the two point in opposite directions. This is **Newton's third law**:

$$
\vec{F}_{\htmlClass{math-hint math-hint-on}{\text{A on B}}} = -\vec{F}_{\text{B on A}}.
$$

<MathHint id="on" label="Subscripts">Read "A on B" as the force that object A exerts on object B. Swapping the names gives the partner force.</MathHint>

The two forces in a pair are equal in size, opposite in direction, and they always act on **different objects**. 

Switch to *Newt pushing a box* and tap the friction link between Newt and Earth. Newt's feet push the floor backward, so the floor pushes Newt forward. That forward push from the floor is the only thing that can move Newt as he exerts his own force on the box.

## Choosing a System

An interaction diagram attempts to show everything; a **free-body diagram** shows the forces acting on a particular object or collection of objects. Draw a dashed **system boundary** around the object or objects you care about.

- A link that **crosses** the boundary is an external interaction. Its force on the system becomes an arrow on the free-body diagram.
- A link with **both ends inside** represents two forces that are internal to the system; neither goes on the diagram.

Tap the bubbles below to move them in or out of the system.

<InteractiveAnchor id="system-boundary" />

<InteractionDiagram client:visible stage="system" initialScene="pushing-box" />

Consider the "Newt pushing a box" scenario.  With only Newt inside the system, the box pushes him backward. If we add the box to the system, that push vanishes from the diagram. Nothing physical changed; the push just became internal. Choosing a clever system is often the fastest way through a problem, because it hides the forces you do not want to solve for.

For simplicity, the system in a free-body diagram is often drawn as a single dot.  Every external force acting on the system is drawn as a vector arrow.  When using these to analyze a problem, be sure to only draw:

- **Forces.** Velocity is not a force. Acceleration is not a force. The quantity $m\vec{a}$ is not a force.
- **Forces acting now.** The shove that started an object moving is not on the diagram once your hand is gone.

Try each of these on Newt. Choose the forces you think belong, look at the diagram you just described, and then check it.

## Newton's First and Second Laws

**Newton's first law** says an object's velocity does not change unless a net force acts on it. This can be seen in [force-free motion](/forces#force-free-newt) when Newt is traveling between walls.  

**Newton's second law** says the combined effect of all forces (i.e. the sum of forces, often called the **net force**) sets the acceleration. 

<ComponentReveal>
  <div slot="vector">
    $$
    \sum \vec{F} = m\vec{a},
    $$
  </div>

  $$
  \begin{aligned}
  \textstyle\sum F_x &= ma_x,\\
  \textstyle\sum F_y &= ma_y.
  \end{aligned}
  $$
</ComponentReveal>

The first law can be considered a special case of the second $\sum \vec{F} = 0$. As in [2D kinematics](/kinematics/two-dimensional), each component equation is independent, provided the axes are perpendicular and stay fixed for the whole problem.

Note that this sum is a *vector* sum.  Newt stands in an elevator that is speeding up as it rises (accelerating upward):

<ComponentReveal label="Diagram">
  <div slot="vector">
    $$
    \vec{N} + m\vec{g} = \sum \vec{F}
    $$
  </div>

  <NetForceDiagram />
</ComponentReveal>

The magnitude of the normal force has to be greater than Newt's weight. If the two were equal, the net force would be zero, and Newt would not accelerate. The small leftover arrow in the vector sum diagram represents the net force (be very careful to **not** think of this as a new force).  It points the way Newt is accelerating: up. 

<ProblemSolvingSection intro="Each of these follows the same path: map the interactions, draw a boundary around a system, then write the second law along each axis.">
  <WorkedExampleCard title="Apparent Weight in an Elevator" accent="blue">
    #### Problem

    A $60\ \text{kg}$ passenger stands on a scale in an elevator accelerating upward at $2.0\ \text{m/s}^2$. What does the scale read? Use $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.

    #### Use

    The system is the passenger. Two links cross the boundary: gravity with Earth and normal with the scale. Taking up as positive,

    $$
    \sum F_y = N - mg = ma_y.
    $$

    #### Solve

    Solving for the normal force,

    $$
    N = m(g + a_y) = (60)(9.8 + 2.0) = 708\ \text{N}.
    $$

    The scale reads the push it receives from the passenger, which is the third-law partner of $N$, so it reads $708\ \text{N}$.

    #### Check

    Larger than the $588\ \text{N}$ weight, as it must be: the net force has to point up to accelerate the passenger up.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard title="Pushing Two Boxes" accent="red">
    #### Problem

    On a frictionless floor, you push box A ($4.0\ \text{kg}$) with a horizontal force of $24\ \text{N}$. Box A pushes box B ($2.0\ \text{kg}$) ahead of it. Find the acceleration and the force box A exerts on box B.

    #### Use

    The interaction diagram has a *push* link between your hand and A, and another *push* link between A and B. Along the floor, nothing else acts. Two different boundaries answer the two questions:

    - Around **A and B together**, the A–B push is internal, so only your $24\ \text{N}$ crosses the boundary.
    - Around **B alone**, only the push from A crosses.

    #### Solve

    For the two-box system,

    $$
    a = \frac{F}{m_A + m_B} = \frac{24}{6.0} = 4.0\ \text{m/s}^2.
    $$

    For box B alone,

    $$
    F_{\text{A on B}} = m_B a = (2.0)(4.0) = 8.0\ \text{N}.
    $$

    #### Check

    On box A alone the forces are $24\ \text{N}$ forward and the $8.0\ \text{N}$ partner backward, and $16\ \text{N} = (4.0)(4.0)$, so both boxes agree on the same acceleration.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard title="Two Blocks and One Rope" accent="blue">
    #### Problem

    A $3.0\ \text{kg}$ block on a frictionless table is connected by a light rope over a pulley to a $2.0\ \text{kg}$ block hanging off the edge. Find the acceleration and the rope tension.

    #### Use

    Draw a boundary around *each* block. The table block has tension $T$ crossing its boundary horizontally; the hanging block has its weight $m_2g$ down and tension $T$ up. The rope links them, so both share one acceleration magnitude $a$:

    $$
    T = m_1a,
    \qquad
    m_2g - T = m_2a.
    $$

    #### Solve

    Adding the two equations eliminates $T$:

    $$
    m_2g = (m_1 + m_2)a
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    a = \frac{(2.0)(9.8)}{5.0} = 3.9\ \text{m/s}^2.
    $$

    Then $T = m_1a = (3.0)(3.9) = 11.8\ \text{N}$.

    #### Check

    Drawing one boundary around both blocks and the rope makes the tension internal and gives $m_2g = (m_1 + m_2)a$ in a single step, the same equation the addition produced.
  </WorkedExampleCard>
</ProblemSolvingSection>

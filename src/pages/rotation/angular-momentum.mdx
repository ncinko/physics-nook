# Angular Momentum

Linear momentum $p = mv$ has a rotational twin, built — as always — by swapping mass for moment of inertia and velocity for angular velocity:

$$
L = I\omega.
$$

**Angular momentum** measures the "quantity of spin," in units of $\text{kg·m}^2/\text{s}$. And just as force is the rate of change of linear momentum, torque is the rate of change of angular momentum:

$$
F = \frac{dp}{dt} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \tau = \frac{dL}{dt}.
$$

This form of Newton's second law is more general than $\tau = I\alpha$, because it still works when the moment of inertia itself changes — and that is where rotation saves its best trick.

## Conservation of Angular Momentum

If the net *external* torque on a system is zero, its angular momentum cannot change:

$$
\tau_{\text{ext}} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad L = I\omega = \text{constant}.
$$

The interesting cases are the ones where $I$ changes while $L$ cannot. A spinning figure skater pulls her arms in: her moment of inertia drops, so her angular velocity *must* rise to keep the product $I\omega$ fixed. No one pushed her. She is not touching anything. The speedup comes entirely from rearranging her own mass.

Keep an eye on the energy readout while pulling the arms in: $L$ holds still, but the kinetic energy $K = \tfrac{1}{2}I\omega^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}L\omega$ *rises* as $\omega$ does. That energy is not free — the skater's muscles do real work hauling her arms inward against the tendency to fly outward. Conservation of angular momentum is not conservation of energy; the two quantities keep separate books.

The same principle steers real machines: divers tuck to somersault faster and stretch out to enter the water cleanly, cats twist their bodies to land feet-first, and spacecraft spin up reaction wheels one way so the craft turns the other.

## Rotational Collisions

Angular momentum conservation also governs the rotational version of a [collision](/collisions). Drop a stationary disk onto a spinning one and friction between the faces drags them to a common angular velocity — an internal torque pair, invisible to the system's total $L$:

$$
I_1\omega_1 = (I_1 + I_2)\,\omega_f
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\omega_f = \frac{I_1\omega_1}{I_1 + I_2}.
$$

This is the perfectly inelastic collision of the rotational world, and it shares that collision's signature: momentum survives, kinetic energy does not. The friction that locks the disks together turns the difference into heat.

  <WorkedExampleCard title="The Skater's Spin-Up" accent="blue">
    #### Problem

    A skater spinning at $3.0\ \text{rad/s}$ with arms out ($I = 4.0\ \text{kg·m}^2$) pulls her arms in, dropping her moment of inertia to $2.0\ \text{kg·m}^2$. Find her new angular speed and the change in kinetic energy.

    #### Use

    No external torque acts about the spin axis, so $I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2$; then compare $K = \tfrac{1}{2}I\omega^2$.

    #### Solve

    $$
    \omega_2 = \frac{I_1\omega_1}{I_2} = \frac{(4.0)(3.0)}{2.0} = 6.0\ \text{rad/s},
    $$

    $$
    K_1 = \tfrac{1}{2}(4.0)(3.0)^2 = 18\ \text{J}, \qquad
    K_2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(2.0)(6.0)^2 = 36\ \text{J}.
    $$

    #### Check

    Halving $I$ doubled $\omega$ — and doubled the kinetic energy, since $K = \tfrac{1}{2}L\omega$ with $L$ fixed. The extra $18\ \text{J}$ is exactly the work her muscles did pulling the arm mass inward.
  </WorkedExampleCard>

  <WorkedExampleCard title="Child on a Merry-Go-Round" accent="red">
    #### Problem

    A playground merry-go-round ($I_{\text{disk}} = 500\ \text{kg·m}^2$) turns at $0.50\ \text{rad/s}$ with a $30\ \text{kg}$ child at its $2.0\ \text{m}$ rim. The child walks to $0.50\ \text{m}$ from the center. Find the new angular speed.

    #### Use

    The child is part of the system, so her $mr^2$ belongs in the total $I$; walking inward changes $I$ but no external torque acts, so $L$ is conserved.

    #### Solve

    $$
    I_1 = 500 + (30)(2.0)^2 = 620\ \text{kg·m}^2, \qquad
    I_2 = 500 + (30)(0.50)^2 = 507.5\ \text{kg·m}^2,
    $$

    $$
    \omega_2 = \frac{I_1\omega_1}{I_2} = \frac{(620)(0.50)}{507.5} \approx 0.61\ \text{rad/s}.
    $$

    #### Check

    The platform speeds up by about $22\%$ — the merry-go-round is the skater's trick at playground scale. If the child walked back out, the spin would slow to exactly $0.50\ \text{rad/s}$ again.
  </WorkedExampleCard>
</ProblemSolvingSection>
